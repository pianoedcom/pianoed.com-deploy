# Piano Terminology Glossary
Music has its own language, and piano has more terms than most. This glossary explains the essential terms in plain, warm language — no gatekeeping, no jargon, just clear explanations for anyone who loves the piano.
## A
### Arpeggio
A broken chord — instead of playing all the notes of a chord at once, you play them one after another, like plucking the strings of a harp. Think of the opening of Beethoven's Moonlight Sonata: that rolling, flowing sound is arpeggios.
### Articulation
How you connect or separate notes. Legato (smooth and connected), staccato (short and detached), and tenuto (held for full value) are all types of articulation. It's the difference between speaking in a whisper and speaking in a shout.
## B
### Bass Clef
The symbol at the beginning of the lower staff in piano music. It tells you that the notes on that staff are in the lower register — played by the left hand. Also called the F clef because its two dots surround the F line.
## C
### Cadence
A musical punctuation mark. Just as a sentence ends with a period, a musical phrase ends with a cadence. A perfect cadence feels like a period; a half cadence feels like a comma — there's more to come.
### Chord
Three or more notes played simultaneously. The building blocks of harmony. A C major chord is C-E-G played together. Most pop songs are built from sequences of chords.
### Crescendo
Gradually getting louder. Written as "cresc." in the score. The opposite is diminuendo (getting softer). Think of it as turning up the emotional intensity.
## D
### Diminuendo
Gradually getting softer. The mirror image of crescendo. Often written as "dim." or shown with a hairpin symbol (an opening or closing angle bracket).
### Dynamics
The volume of music — how loud or soft you play. From pianissimo (very soft) to fortissimo (very loud), dynamics are the emotional palette of the piano.
## F
### Forte
Loud. Written as "f" in the score. The piano's full name — "fortepiano" — means "loud-soft," because it could finally do both, unlike the harpsichord before it.
## L
### Legato
Smooth and connected. Notes flow into each other without gaps. The opposite of staccato. Legato playing is what gives piano music its singing quality.
## M
### Metronome
A device that produces a steady beat at a chosen tempo. Essential for developing a sense of rhythm and for practicing at controlled speeds. Measured in BPM (beats per minute).
## P
### Pedal
The sustain pedal (right pedal) lifts the dampers off all strings, allowing notes to ring and blend together. The "soul" of the piano — it's what makes the instrument sing.
### Pianissimo
Very soft. Written as "pp" in the score. The quietest common dynamic marking. Playing pianissimo with control is one of the hardest skills on piano.
### Piano
Soft. The instrument's name comes from its ability to play softly (piano) as well as loudly (forte). Also, of course, the name of the instrument itself.
## R
### Rubato
"Stolen time." A flexible approach to tempo where you push and pull the speed for expressive purposes. Chopin was the master of rubato — his music breathes with it.
## S
### Staccato
Short and detached. Notes are played briefly with a gap between them. The opposite of legato. Think of it as musical tap dancing.
### Sustain Pedal
See Pedal. The right pedal on a piano. When pressed, it sustains all notes being played, creating a rich, blended sound.
## T
### Tempo
The speed of the music. Measured in BPM (beats per minute). Tempo markings in Italian (Allegro, Andante, Adagio) give both a speed range and a character indication.
### Treble Clef
The symbol at the beginning of the upper staff in piano music. It indicates the higher register, played by the right hand. Also called the G clef.
## V
### Voicing
How you distribute notes within a chord — which note is on top, which is in the middle, which is on the bottom. Good voicing is what separates amateur from professional playing. In jazz, voicing is everything.
