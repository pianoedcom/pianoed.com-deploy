One of the most common concerns aspiring pianists have is: "My hands are too small to play the piano well." It's a understandable worry — we've all seen videos of Rachmaninoff's enormous hands spanning 12 keys, and concert pianists with fingers that seem to stretch effortlessly across the keyboard.
But here's the truth: some of the greatest pianists in history had small hands. Alicia de Larrocha could barely reach an octave, yet she was the definitive interpreter of Granados and Falla. Josef Hofmann had small hands and had his Steinway adjusted with a narrower key width. Small hands are not a disqualification — they're a condition you learn to work with.
Here are practical technique tips, exercises, and repertoire suggestions for pianists with smaller hands.
## What "Small Hands" Means
An average adult hand span (thumb to pinky, fully stretched) is about 8.5 inches. A comfortable octave span requires about 7.5 inches. If your span is less than that, octaves may feel strained, and large [chords](/blog/how-to-play-piano-by-ear) will require creative solutions.
<Callout type="note">
If you can comfortably reach an octave (7 white keys from thumb to pinky), you can play the vast majority of piano repertoire. If you can reach a 9th, you can play nearly everything. Many great pianists have spans of exactly one octave.
</Callout>
## Technique Adjustments for Small Hands
### 1. Use the Pedal for Sustained Notes
When a chord is too wide to hold, play the notes you can reach and let the sustain pedal hold the rest. This is not cheating — it's what every pianist does. The pedal is your second pair of hands.
### 2. Roll Large Chords
When a chord has more notes than you can hold, roll it — play the notes quickly from bottom to top rather than simultaneously. This is a standard technique used by pianists of all hand sizes, and many composers write "arpeggiato" (rolled) markings into their scores.
### 3. Redistribute Between Hands
If the left hand has a large chord and the right hand is free, borrow a note. Play the bottom note of the chord with the right hand if it's reachable. This is a common solution in Romantic repertoire where the left hand has wide-spanning chords.
### 4. Use [Finger](/blog/best-piano-finger-exercises-for-beginners) Substitution
In passages where you need to hold a note while reaching for another, use finger substitution — swap one finger for another on the same key without releasing it. This lets you "extend" your reach without actually stretching.
### 5. Rotate the Wrist
Small-handed pianists benefit from more wrist [rotation](/blog/how-to-play-piano-with-small-hands). Instead of trying to reach with stiff fingers, rotate the forearm to bring the hand into position. This reduces strain and increases effective reach.
### 6. Practice Relaxation
Tension is the enemy of small hands. When you stretch, the tendency is to tense up — which actually reduces your reach. Practice consciously relaxing your hand, wrist, and forearm. A relaxed hand reaches further than a tense one.
<Callout type="tip">
Before attempting large stretches, warm up thoroughly. Cold hands are less flexible and more prone to injury. See our [Piano Warm-Up Exercises Checklist](/blog/piano-warm-up-exercises-checklist) for a complete routine.
</Callout>
## Exercises to Increase Flexibility
### Gentle Stretching
- **Finger spread** — Place your hand flat on a table, gently spread your fingers apart, hold 10 seconds, relax. Repeat 5 times.
- **Thumb-pinky stretch** — Gently stretch your thumb and pinky apart as far as comfortable, hold 10 seconds. Never force — stop if you feel pain.
- **Web stretch** — Gently pull the webbing between your thumb and index finger to stretch that area.
<Callout type="warning">
Never stretch to the point of pain. Stretching should feel like a gentle pull, never sharp or burning. If you feel pain, stop immediately. Consistency is more important than intensity — gentle stretching daily is far more effective than aggressive stretching occasionally.
</Callout>
### Technical Exercises
- **Octave practice** — Play octaves slowly, focusing on relaxation rather than speed. Start with C-C, then move chromatically up and down.
- **Broken octaves** — Play C-G-C-G (root-fifth-root-fifth) [patterns](/blog/how-to-practice-piano-hands-separately) to develop the reach between 1-5 and 1-3.
- **Hanon with extensions** — Modify Hanon exercises to include wider intervals as your flexibility improves.
## Repertoire Friendly to Small Hands
### Easy (Beginner–Early Intermediate)
- **Bach** — Two-Part Inventions (most fit comfortably in small hands)
- **Mozart** — Easy Sonatas (K.545, K.283) — Mozart rarely writes chords wider than an octave
- **Schumann** — "Album for the Young" — short pieces with manageable spans
- **Debussy** — "Children's Corner Suite" — accessible and beautiful
### Intermediate
- **Chopin** — Nocturnes (most are playable with an octave span), Preludes (Op. 28)
- **Schubert** — Impromptus (Op. 90) — manageable spans, gorgeous music
- **Grieg** — Lyric Pieces — short, charming, small-hand friendly
- **Brahms** — Selected Intermezzi (Op. 116–119) — intimate and technically accessible
### Advanced (with adjustments)
- **Beethoven** — Most sonatas are playable with an octave span, though some late sonatas require creative redistribution
- **Debussy** — Préludes — some require wide spans but can be rolled or redistributed
- **Ravel** — "Pavane pour une infante défunte" — beautiful and mostly manageable
### Repertoire to Approach with Caution
- **Rachmaninoff** — Large spans are common; many chords require a 10th or more
- **Liszt** — Virtuoso repertoire often demands large spans
- **Brahms** — Late sonatas and some variation sets have wide chords
- **Alkan** — Notoriously large-handed repertoire
<Callout type="note">
Even in repertoire with large chords, you can almost always find an edition with the chords rolled or redistributed. Don't avoid a piece you love because of its reputation — look for solutions and ask a teacher for help.
</Callout>
## The Psychological Side
The biggest obstacle for small-handed pianists is often psychological, not physical. You hear a piece requires a 10th and immediately cross it off your list. But:
- Most repertoire does not require spans larger than an octave
- Large chords can almost always be rolled or redistributed
- Many "impossible" pieces have been recorded by pianists with small hands
- Your musical voice [matters](/blog/how-to-memorize-piano-pieces-faster) more than your hand size
Alicia de Larrocha said it best: "My hands are small, but my heart is big." That's the spirit. Your hands are the ones you have — learn to use them well, and they'll take you further than you think.
