The Baroque era, stretching roughly from 1600 to 1750, is the period in which the piano was born — though the instrument we recognize today was still taking its first breaths. This was an age of ornate ornamentation, figured bass, and the towering keyboard works of Johann Sebastian Bach, George Frideric Handel, and Domenico Scarlatti. Yet for most of the Baroque, the dominant [keyboard instruments](/blog/baroque-keyboard-music-history-guide) were the harpsichord, the clavichord, and the organ. The piano, or *fortepiano* as it was first called, arrived late in the period as a quiet revolution.
Understanding the piano's Baroque origins helps us appreciate not only the instrument's mechanical ingenuity but also the musical culture that shaped its earliest repertoire. This guide traces that story warmly, welcoming newcomers and enthusiasts alike into a fascinating chapter of musical history.
## The World Before the Piano
For most of the Baroque era, keyboardists had no way to change dynamic level by touch alone. The harpsichord plucked its strings with a consistent force, meaning a player could shape phrases through articulation and timing but not through volume. The clavichord, more intimate and expressive, allowed some dynamic shading but was far too quiet to be heard alongside other instruments.
This limitation frustrated musicians and instrument makers alike. Composers wrote terraced dynamics — sudden shifts between loud and soft — because gradual crescendos were simply impossible on the instruments available. The musical world was ready for an instrument that could sing with nuance, responding to the player's fingers.
<Callout type="info">
The word "Baroque" likely comes from the Portuguese *barroco*, meaning an irregular pearl — a fitting image for a period defined by ornate detail and expressive ornamentation.
</Callout>
## Bartolomeo Cristofori and the Birth of the Fortepiano
Around the year 1700, an Italian instrument maker named Bartolomeo Cristofori, employed by the Medici court in Florence, solved the harpsichord's great limitation. His invention used hammers that struck the strings and then fell back, allowing the player to control volume through the force of the keystroke. He called it the *gravicembalo col piano e forte* — "harpsichord with soft and loud" — which was soon shortened to *fortepiano* or simply *piano*.
Cristofori's mechanism was remarkably sophisticated. It included an escapement that let the hammer fall away from the string after striking, even if the key remained held down, and a check to prevent the hammer from bouncing back. Only three of his instruments survive today, but they remain marvels of craftsmanship.
The new instrument spread slowly. It was expensive, delicate, and unfamiliar. Many musicians of the time were skeptical, and for decades the harpsichord remained the dominant keyboard instrument in both concert and home settings.
## Bach, Handel, and the Baroque Keyboard [Masters](/blog/famous-classical-pianists-who-changed-music)
Johann Sebastian Bach reportedly encountered one of Cristofori's pianos in 1736, late in his life, at the court of Frederick the Great in Potsdam. He was impressed but did not compose extensively for the instrument, dedicating his genius instead to the harpsichord and organ. His keyboard works — the *Well-Tempered Clavier*, the *Goldberg Variations*, the English and French Suites — form the cornerstone of Baroque keyboard literature and remain central to every pianist's training.
George Frideric Handel, though best known for his operas and oratorios, also wrote substantial keyboard music, including suites and chaconnes designed for the harpsichord. His works emphasize grand gestures and harmonic richness.
Domenico Scarlatti, son of the famous opera composer Alessandro Scarlatti, wrote over 550 keyboard sonatas that dazzle with their virtuosity, rhythmic vitality, and Iberian flair. Though conceived for the harpsichord, Scarlatti's sonatas translate beautifully to the modern piano and are beloved by [performers](/blog/piano-concerto-history-guide) and audiences alike.
## How Baroque Music Translates to the Modern Piano
Playing Baroque music on a modern grand piano presents both opportunities and challenges. The modern instrument's sustained tone, wide dynamic range, and sustaining pedal offer expressive possibilities that Baroque composers never imagined — but they can also obscure the clarity that this music demands.
Pianists generally approach Baroque repertoire with restraint, using the pedal sparingly and favoring clean, articulated touch. The goal is to honor the music's contrapuntal transparency while still taking advantage of the piano's singing tone. Many pianists study these works on the harpsichord at least once, to understand the articulations and phrasing the composers originally intended.
## The Legacy of the Baroque Piano
Though the Baroque era ended with Bach's death in 1750, the seeds Cristofori planted would blossom in the [Classical](/blog/best-piano-recordings-of-all-time) period that followed. The piano's capacity for dynamic expression would inspire Haydn, Mozart, and Beethoven to write music of unprecedented emotional range. For more on that transition, see our [piano sonata history guide](/blog/piano-sonata-history-guide) and our broader [history of the piano evolution](/blog/history-of-the-piano-evolution).
The Baroque era gave the piano its birth and its first repertoire. Every time a pianist sits down to play a Bach prelude or a Scarlatti sonata, they carry forward a tradition that began in the candlelit courts of 18th-century Europe — a tradition of invention, expression, and the enduring human desire to make the keyboard sing.
