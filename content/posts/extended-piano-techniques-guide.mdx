For most of its history, the piano has been played one way: by pressing keys that send hammers to strike strings. But in the twentieth and twenty-first centuries, composers and performers have asked what else the piano can do. The answer is a rich world of extended techniques — plucking the strings, bowing them, muting them, striking the frame, using the body of the piano as a resonant object. These techniques expand the piano from a keyboard instrument into a full sound source, and they have become an essential part of [contemporary piano music](/blog/contemporary-piano-music-genres-overview).
## What Are Extended Techniques?
Extended techniques are methods of playing an instrument beyond its conventional use. For the piano, that means anything other than pressing keys to strike strings in the usual way. They include:
- Plucking (pizzicato) the strings directly
- Bowing the strings with various materials
- Strumming the strings like a harp or guitar
- Muting or stopping the strings with the hand or objects
- Striking the strings, frame, or case
- Using the pedals in unconventional ways
- Reaching inside the piano to manipulate the strings or dampers
These techniques produce sounds the standard keyboard cannot — harp-like plucks, eerie sustained drones, percussive strikes, and ghostly harmonics — vastly expanding the piano's sonic palette.
<Callout type="note">
Extended techniques are not gimmicks. In the hands of skilled composers and performers, they reveal the piano as a complex resonant body with far more voices than its keyboard suggests. They are part of the instrument's [modern](/blog/piano-etude-history-guide) vocabulary.
</Callout>
## A Brief History
Extended piano techniques emerged prominently in the twentieth century, as composers sought new sounds and challenged conventional instrumental identity. The [prepared piano](/blog/prepared-piano-techniques-guide) of John Cage was one early and influential example. From there, composers explored ever more direct ways of interacting with the piano's strings and body.
The rise of [twentieth-century piano music](/blog/piano-in-20th-century-music-guide) and the experimental tradition brought extended techniques into the mainstream of contemporary concert music. Today, they appear in solo repertoire, chamber music, [film scores](/blog/piano-cultural-impact-on-modern-music), and improvisation.
## Common Extended Techniques
### Plucking the Strings (Pizzicato)
The pianist reaches inside the piano and plucks the strings directly with a finger or plectrum. The result is a harp- or guitar-like tone, far more delicate and sustained than a hammered note.
- **Direct plucking** — using the fingertip or fingernail
- **Plectrum plucking** — using a guitar pick or similar for a sharper attack
- **With pedal** — holding the sustain pedal lets plucked notes ring freely
Plucking allows precise control of dynamics and articulation impossible from the keyboard. The position along the string changes the tone and the availability of harmonics.
### Bowing the Strings
Bowing a piano string with a bow (a violin or cello bow, or a custom bow) produces a sustained, eerie tone unlike anything the hammers can create. The string must be accessible — usually requiring the piano to be open — and the bowing technique adapted to the string's length and tension.
- **Rosined bows** — produce a sustained singing tone
- **Other materials** — fishing line, strips of material, or custom bows create different textures
Bowed piano is haunting and sustained, often used for atmospheric and drone-like passages.
### Strumming the Strings
The pianist strums the strings with a finger or plectrum, like a harp or guitar. This produces glissando-like sweeps or chordal strums with a plucked [character](/blog/piano-in-film-scoring-history). Combined with the sustain pedal, strumming creates lush, resonant textures.
### Muting and Stopping the Strings
The pianist touches the strings with a finger or object while the note plays, changing the tone:
- **Light muting** — damps the vibration, producing a softer, more percussive sound
- **Harmonic stopping** — lightly touching a node produces harmonics, ringing overtones
- **Full stopping** — firmly stopping the string changes the pitch or silences it
Harmonics are especially magical — touching the string at specific points produces pure, flute-like tones an octave or more above the fundamental.
### Striking the Strings, Frame, or Case
Percussive strikes on the strings, the metal frame, or the wooden case add a battery of drum-like sounds:
- **String strikes** — hitting strings with a mallet or beater for a sharp, percussive attack
- **Frame strikes** — striking the metal frame for metallic, bell-like tones
- **Case strikes** — tapping or knocking the wooden case for drum-like effects
These transform the piano into a percussion instrument alongside its keyboard role.
### Cluster and Palm Techniques
Some composers ask the pianist to play with the palm, forearm, or a flat hand to produce tone clusters — dense masses of sound impossible with individual fingers. This is a keyboard technique but extends conventional fingering dramatically.
### Unconventional Pedaling
- **Half-pedaling** — releasing the pedal partway to partially clear the resonance
- **Sostenuto pedal** — sustaining only selected notes while others are unaffected
- **Rapid pedaling** — fluttering the pedal for shimmering effects
These are keyboard-adjacent techniques that exploit the pedal mechanism in unusual ways.
## Playing Inside the Piano
Many extended techniques require reaching inside the piano — which means the lid must be open (for grands) or the front removed (for uprights). This demands care:
- **Mind the action** — don't interfere with the hammers, dampers, or moving parts
- **Watch the strings** — sharp fingernails or rings can scratch or damage strings
- **Protect the finish** — resting on the case can scratch it; use a cloth if needed
- **Be gentle** — the strings and frame are under high tension; never force anything
<Callout type="warning">
Extended techniques can damage a piano if done carelessly. Use a piano you can experiment with, not a treasured or valuable instrument. Never force objects between strings, and always confirm the piano returns to normal playing condition afterward.
</Callout>
## Notating Extended Techniques
Composers have developed notation conventions for extended techniques since there's no universal standard:
- **Specific instructions** — many scores include written instructions describing each technique in detail
- **Symbols** — some composers use custom symbols, explained in a legend
- **Preparation-style charts** — for techniques requiring specific setups, a chart may accompany the score
Performers must read the composer's instructions carefully, as the same symbol can mean different things in different scores.
## Extended Techniques in Performance
Performing extended piano music requires:
- **Familiarity with the piano's interior** — knowing where strings, dampers, and nodes are
- **Physical comfort reaching inside** — grand pianos are more accessible than uprights
- **Care and control** — precise, gentle movements to avoid damage
- **Coordination with keyboard playing** — many pieces combine extended techniques with conventional playing, sometimes simultaneously
- **A suitable instrument** — some techniques work better on grands than uprights, and some venues restrict interior access to protect their pianos
## Extended Techniques Across Genres
Though rooted in contemporary concert music, extended techniques appear in other settings:
- **Film and game scores** — eerie bowed strings and ghostly harmonics suit atmospheric music
- **Improvisation and jazz** — improvising pianists use selective extended techniques for color
- **Experimental and ambient music** — the piano's resonant body suits drone and texture-based work
- **Cross-genre exploration** — artists blend extended techniques with [popular](/blog/piano-in-film-and-popular-culture) and world music traditions
The techniques have spread far beyond their avant-garde origins.
## Trying Extended Techniques
For curious pianists, exploring extended techniques is rewarding but should be approached carefully:
1. **Use a piano you can experiment with** — not a treasured instrument
2. **Start gently** — plucking and light muting are safer than bowing or striking
3. **Explore harmonics** — lightly touching a string at its midpoint while the key plays produces a pure harmonic; a magical first discovery
4. **Combine with the pedal** — let plucked or bowed notes ring
5. **Read scores with extended techniques** — to see how composers specify them
6. **Remove all objects and confirm normal playing** afterward
The first time you hear a piano string ring as a harmonic, or feel the resonance of a bowed string under your hand, the instrument feels new — full of sounds you never knew were there.
## The Extended Piano and the Instrument's Future
Extended techniques are part of a broader story: the piano as a living, evolving instrument. From its [evolution](/blog/evolution-of-piano-design-through-centuries) through the centuries to the [hybrid and digital pianos](/blog/how-do-hybrid-pianos-work-explained) of today, the piano has always been reshaped by the people who play it. Extended techniques reshape it not through design but through use — revealing that the instrument's body holds far more music than its keyboard alone suggests.
Extended techniques are an invitation to listen to the piano anew. They remind us that the most refined and traditional of [instruments](/blog/baroque-keyboard-music-history-guide) still has unexplored voices — and that the boundary between the conventional and the experimental is, like the strings themselves, there to be touched.
