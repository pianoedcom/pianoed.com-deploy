The cadenza is one of the most thrilling and distinctive features of the piano's [classical](/blog/best-piano-recordings-of-all-time) repertoire. It is that moment in a concerto when the orchestra falls silent and the soloist is left alone at the keyboard, free to dazzle, to reflect, and to improvise. Originally a space for spontaneous invention, the cadenza evolved from a brief improvisational flourish into elaborate written-out showpieces that test a pianist's virtuosity and musicianship. Understanding the cadenza — its history, its function, and how to approach it — opens a window into the relationship between composer, performer, and audience that has defined [classical](/blog/famous-classical-pianists-who-changed-music) music for centuries.
This guide explores the cadenza's history, examines notable examples, and offers guidance for pianists who want to understand, interpret, or even create their own cadenzas.
## The Origins of the Cadenza
The cadenza emerged in the Classical era as part of the concerto form. In a concerto, the soloist and orchestra are partners — sometimes cooperating, sometimes contrasting. Near the end of the first movement, the orchestra pauses on a chord known as the six-four chord, and the soloist takes over. In the early Classical period, this was an opportunity for the performer to improvise — to show off their inventiveness and technique, to elaborate on the movement's themes, and to build anticipation before the final orchestral resolution.
The word "cadenza" comes from the Italian for "cadence," reflecting its original function: an elaborate cadential flourish that led into the movement's conclusion. The performer was expected to improvise, drawing on the movement's themes but free to add virtuosic display. This improvisational [tradition](/blog/baroque-keyboard-music-history-guide) connected the cadenza to the older practice of figured-bass realization and the improvisatory skills expected of all classical musicians.
## The Classical Cadenza
In the hands of Mozart and his contemporaries, the cadenza became a more defined but still improvisational space.
### Mozart's Improvised Cadenzas
Mozart, a brilliant improviser, wrote cadenzas for his own concertos but expected other performers to improvise their own. His cadenzas typically reference the movement's main themes, weaving them into a virtuosic display that builds to a trill — the signal that the cadenza is ending and the orchestra is about to return. The cadenza was both a showpiece and a structural device, prolonging the tension before the final resolution.
### The Performer's Role
In the Classical era, the cadenza was the performer's domain. A pianist was expected to be able to improvise a convincing cadenza on the spot, demonstrating both technical command and musical imagination. This expectation placed great demands on performers and reflected a musical culture in which improvisation was a core skill, not a specialty.
## The [Beethoven Transformation](/blog/piano-concerto-history-guide)
Beethoven changed the cadenza's role profoundly.
### Written-Out Cadenzas
As the Classical era gave way to the Romantic, Beethoven began writing out cadenzas himself, removing the improvisational element. For his later concertos, he composed specific cadenzas, treating them as integral parts of the work rather than performer's prerogatives. This reflected a broader shift: as music became more complex and [composers](/blog/romantic-era-piano-composers-guide) asserted more control, the performer's freedom to improvise diminished. The cadenza became a composed showpiece rather than an improvised one.
### The Emperor Concerto
In his Fifth Piano Concerto, the "Emperor," Beethoven went further still — he wrote the cadenza into the score with the orchestra accompanying, eliminating the free improvisational cadenza entirely. This was a radical step that signaled the end of the improvised cadenza in the grand concerto tradition. The performer's role shifted from creator to interpreter, executing the composer's vision rather than inventing their own.
## Romantic and Beyond
The Romantic era embraced the cadenza as a virtuosic showcase, even as it became fully composed.
### Liszt and the Virtuoso Cadenza
Liszt and other Romantic virtuosi wrote elaborate cadenzas that pushed the piano's technical limits. These cadenzas were designed to thrill audiences with cascading runs, enormous chords, and dazzling display. The cadenza became a highlight of the concerto, a moment for the soloist to shine alone and for the audience to marvel. Even when composed, the cadenza retained the spirit of improvisation — the illusion of spontaneous invention.
### Cadenzas in Solo Works
The cadenza also appeared in solo works, not just concertos. [Chopin](/blog/great-piano-competitions-history-guide), Liszt, and others inserted cadenza-like passages into sonatas, ballades, and other solo pieces — moments of free, virtuosic writing that interrupt the formal structure. These solo cadenzas served similar purposes: display, reflection, and structural contrast.
## Notable Cadenza Examples
A few cadenzas are landmarks of the repertoire.
### Mozart's C Minor Concerto
Mozart's Piano Concerto No. 24 in C minor contains one of the most dramatic cadenzas in the Classical repertoire. The cadenza (whether improvised in Mozart's time or composed later) wrestles with the concerto's dark, intense themes, building enormous tension before the final resolution. It is a model of how a cadenza can deepen a work's emotional character.
### Beethoven's Emperor Concerto
The "Emperor" concerto's written-in cadenza, with orchestra, shows the fully composed cadenza at its grandest. The soloist and orchestra together create a culminating display that is both virtuosic and structurally integrated. It represents the apex of the cadenza's evolution into a composed element.
### Brahms's Cadenzas
Brahms wrote cadenzas for several Mozart and Beethoven concertos, offering a Romantic-era composer's take on the Classical cadenza. His cadenzas are rich, complex, and deeply respectful of the original works while adding Brahms's harmonic and virtuosic voice. They are valuable both as performances and as windows into how one great composer reimagined another's work.
<Callout type="note" title="Improvising Your Own Cadenza">
For pianists interested in the improvisational tradition, writing or improvising your own cadenza for a Mozart or early Beethoven concerto is a rewarding exercise. Study the movement's themes, identify the harmonic points of arrival, and craft a cadenza that develops the themes virtuosically and leads to the concluding trill. It connects you to the improvisational culture that gave birth to the cadenza.
</Callout>
## Approaching the Cadenza as a Performer
Whether improvised or composed, the cadenza demands special attention.
### Structural Understanding
Understand the cadenza's role in the movement's structure. It typically prolongs the dominant harmony, building tension before the final tonic resolution. Know where the cadenza begins and ends harmonically, and shape it to build toward its climax and resolution. A cadenza that meanders loses its dramatic function.
### Technical Command
Cadenzas are often the most technically demanding passages in a concerto. They may contain runs, leaps, octaves, and intricate passagework. Master the technique thoroughly, but remember that technique serves expression — a cadenza played with flawless technique but no musical shape is empty. Shape every phrase, every crescendo and diminuendo, to tell a story.
### The Concluding Trill
Most cadenzas end with a trill, the signal to the orchestra to re-enter. This trill is not just a technical device but a dramatic moment — it is the culmination of the cadenza's tension and the bridge back to the orchestral tutti. Shape the trill to build intensity, then release it as the orchestra returns. The trill is the cadenza's punctuation mark; make it count.
## Writing Your Own Cadenza
For the adventurous pianist, composing a cadenza is a deep engagement with a composer's style.
### Study the Themes
Begin by studying the movement's themes thoroughly. A cadenza should draw on these themes, transforming and combining them. Identify the most characteristic motives and think about how to develop them virtuosically.
### Follow the Harmony
The cadenza must begin and end at the right harmonic points — typically starting after the six-four chord and ending on the dominant, leading to the tonic resolution. Plan your harmonic journey to arrive at the right place at the right time. The cadenza is free in many ways, but its harmonic frame is fixed.
### Build to a Climax
A good cadenza builds in intensity toward its climax, then resolves. Shape the dynamics, the register, and the technical demands to create a satisfying arc. The listener should feel the tension build and release, even within the cadenza's freedom.
## Conclusion
The cadenza is a remarkable feature of the piano's classical tradition — a moment where the performer stands alone, where virtuosity and expression meet, and where the relationship between composer and performer is most visible. From its improvisational origins to its fully composed Romantic form, the cadenza has evolved while retaining its essential dramatic function. Understanding its history and examples deepens your interpretation of the concertos and solo works that contain cadenzas, and for the adventurous, writing your own connects you to the improvisational tradition that gave the cadenza birth. The cadenza remains one of the piano's most thrilling moments, a space for the soloist to shine and for the music to soar.
