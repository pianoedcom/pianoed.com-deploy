When pianists talk about technique exercises, Hanon usually dominates the conversation. The Virtuoso Pianist is ubiquitous in piano education, and its exercises are practiced by millions of pianists worldwide. But there's another composer of piano exercises whose work is, in many ways, more musically rewarding and technically comprehensive: Carl Czerny. His exercises are less famous than Hanon's, but among serious pianists and teachers, they're often considered the superior choice for developing well-rounded technique.
Czerny's exercises differ from Hanon's in a fundamental way: they're musical. Where Hanon's exercises are pure finger patterns with no melodic or harmonic interest, Czerny's exercises are actual pieces of music — short, yes, but with melodic phrases, harmonic progressions, and dynamic markings. This means that when you practice Czerny, you're not just [building](/blog/classical-piano-repertoire-by-era-guide) finger technique — you're also developing musicality, phrasing, and dynamic control. You're practicing two things at once, which makes your practice time more efficient and more enjoyable.
## Who Was Carl Czerny?
Carl Czerny (1791–1857) was an Austrian pianist, composer, and teacher whose position in music history is unique. He was a student of Beethoven — the great composer recognized Czerny's talent and taught him for several years, focusing on performance and composition. Czerny was also the teacher of Liszt — the young Franz Liszt studied with Czerny and credited him with laying the technical foundation that enabled his legendary virtuosity. In between these two giants of piano history, Czerny built a career as one of the most respected piano pedagogues of the 19th century.
Czerny's pedagogical philosophy was rooted in the belief that technique should serve musical expression. Unlike some of his contemporaries who advocated for mechanical finger exercises, Czerny believed that technical development should happen within a musical context. His exercises are designed to build specific technical skills — scale passages, arpeggios, [trills](/blog/piano-ornamentation-and-trills-guide), octaves, broken chords — but they do so within short pieces that have melodic shape and harmonic direction. This approach means that the technique you develop is immediately applicable to real repertoire.
Why his exercises endure is a testament to their effectiveness. While many 19th-century exercise books have been forgotten, Czerny's collections remain in print and in use because they work. They develop technique systematically, they're musical enough to be enjoyable, and they progress logically from simple to complex. Generations of pianists — from Liszt to modern conservatory students — have built their technique on Czerny's foundation.
<Callout>
**Czerny's legacy**: Czerny composed over 1,000 works, including symphonies, string quartets, and piano sonatas. But it's his pedagogical works — the exercises and études — that have ensured his lasting influence. Every pianist who has played a Czerny exercise is part of an unbroken teaching lineage stretching back to Beethoven.
</Callout>
## Op. 599: The Practical Method for Beginners
Opus 599, "Practical Method for Beginners on the Pianoforte," is Czerny's most accessible collection and the best starting point for pianists new to his exercises. It contains 100 short exercises that progress from the simplest five-finger patterns to more complex passages involving scales, arpeggios, and broken chords. Each exercise is a complete short piece, usually 8–16 measures long, with a clear melodic line and simple harmonic accompaniment.
The [structure](/blog/best-piano-method-books-compared) and progression of Op. 599 is carefully designed. The first exercises (No. 1–10) focus on simple five-finger patterns in C major, with the right hand playing the melody and the left hand providing simple chordal accompaniment. As you progress through the collection, new elements are introduced: scale passages (No. 20–30), arpeggio patterns (No. 40–50), broken chord figurations (No. 60–70), and more advanced combinations (No. 80–100). Each exercise builds on the skills developed in the previous ones.
Key exercises to start with include No. 1 (basic five-finger position, both hands), No. 6 (introduction of the thumb crossing), No. 11 (scale passages in C major), and No. 18 (broken chord patterns). These exercises cover the fundamental technical vocabulary that every pianist needs. Practice them slowly, with attention to evenness, fingering, and musical phrasing. Don't rush through them — each exercise has something specific to teach.
How it compares to Hanon: Op. 599 is both more musical and more comprehensive than Hanon's Virtuoso Pianist. Where Hanon repeats the same pattern in every key (which is efficient but monotonous), Czerny introduces new musical and technical [challenges](/blog/piano-repertoire-for-small-hands) in each exercise. The trade-off is that Czerny takes longer to cover the same technical ground — but what you lose in speed, you gain in musicality and engagement. Many teachers recommend using both: Hanon for pure finger conditioning, Czerny for musical technique development.
## Op. 849: The School of Velocity
Opus 849, "The School of Velocity," is Czerny's intermediate-level collection, designed to be studied after Op. 599. It contains 30 exercises that focus on speed, agility, and evenness. The exercises are longer and more complex than those in Op. 599, with more challenging technical demands and more sophisticated musical content.
When to move to this collection depends on your progress through Op. 599. A good rule of thumb is to begin Op. 849 when you've completed about 60–70 exercises from Op. 599 and are comfortable with scale passages, arpeggios, and broken chords. Starting Op. 849 too early — before your fundamental technique is secure — can lead to tension and bad habits as you try to play faster than your technique allows.
The exercises for finger agility in Op. 849 focus on rapid passage work, scale fragments, and broken chord patterns that require quick, even finger work. Exercise No. 1 is a classic speed-building study: a simple five-finger pattern that moves through different keys, requiring rapid, even playing. Exercise No. 3 focuses on scale passages with thumb crossings. Exercise No. 8 features broken chord patterns that require flexible wrist movement.
Tempo and practice approach for Op. 849 should be deliberate. These exercises are designed to build speed, but speed should never come at the expense of evenness and control. Start at a tempo where you can play perfectly — usually around 80–100 BPM playing sixteenth notes. Focus on evenness, clarity, and relaxation. Only increase the tempo when you can play cleanly at the current speed. Rushing through these exercises at a tempo you can't handle will reinforce tension and unevenness — the opposite of what they're designed to achieve.
<Note>
**Practice tempo**: Czerny indicated tempos for many of his exercises, but these reflect the performance standards of his era. Modern pianists should focus on control rather than speed. A perfectly even performance at a moderate tempo is more valuable than a fast, sloppy one. The speed will come naturally as your technique develops.
</Note>
## Op. 740: The Art of Finger Dexterity
Opus 740, "The Art of Finger Dexterity," is Czerny's advanced collection, containing 50 exercises that demand a high level of technical proficiency. These exercises are essentially études — concert-level pieces that happen to focus on specific technical challenges. They're comparable in [difficulty](/blog/classical-piano-sheet-music-for-beginners) to Chopin's Études, though without the same level of musical genius.
The advanced technique building in Op. 740 covers the full range of piano technique: rapid octaves, chord passages, double notes, trills, repeated notes, and complex polyrhythms. Each exercise isolates one or two technical challenges and develops them comprehensively within a musical context. The exercises are longer than those in Op. 599 or Op. 849 — often 2–4 pages — and require sustained concentration and physical stamina.
Musicality within technical exercises is a hallmark of Op. 740. These are not dry finger calisthenics — they're expressive pieces with dynamic contrasts, rubato indications, and emotional character. Czerny wanted pianists to develop technique and musicality simultaneously, and Op. 740 is his most complete realization of that philosophy. When practicing these exercises, pay attention to the dynamic markings, the phrasing, and the character indications — they're an integral part of the exercise, not optional embellishments.
Selecting exercises for specific needs is one of the advantages of Op. 740's comprehensive scope. If you need to work on octaves, practice No. 3, No. 17, and No. 31. If trills are your weakness, focus on No. 7 and No. 22. If you're preparing a piece with rapid broken chords, practice No. 11 and No. 28. The collection is a technical toolkit — you can select the exercises that address your specific weaknesses rather than working through the entire collection in order.
## Building a Czerny Practice Routine
A Czerny practice routine should be integrated into your daily practice, not treated as a separate activity. The exercises serve as both warm-up and technique development, preparing your hands for the repertoire you'll practice afterward. How many exercises per session depends on your level and goals — a good starting point is 2–3 exercises from your current collection, practiced for 10–15 minutes total.
Combining Czerny with other methods creates a well-rounded technique routine. You might start with 5 minutes of scales and arpeggios (for pure finger conditioning), then 10 minutes of Czerny (for musical technique), then 5 minutes of Hanon (for targeted finger independence), and then move on to your repertoire. This combination covers all the bases: pure technique, musical technique, and repertoire application.
Tracking progress through the collections is important for maintaining motivation and ensuring systematic development. Keep a practice journal noting which exercises you've worked on, at what tempo, and what challenges you encountered. When you can play an exercise cleanly at the indicated tempo (or at a tempo you've set as your goal), mark it as complete and move to the next. Don't skip exercises — each one develops a specific skill that later exercises build upon.
The goal of Czerny practice is not to play all 100 exercises of Op. 599 or all 30 of Op. 849. The goal is to develop the technique that these exercises are designed to build. If you find that after 40 exercises of Op. 599 your technique has improved to the point where the remaining exercises feel easy, it's time to move to Op. 849. The exercises are a means to an end, not an end in themselves. Use them to build the technique you need for the music you want to play — that's what Czerny intended.
