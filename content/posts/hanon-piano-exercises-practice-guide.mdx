Love them or hate them, Hanon exercises are part of every serious pianist's training. Charles-Louis Hanon's "The Virtuoso Pianist in 60 Exercises" has been controversial since its publication in 1873 — some teachers swear by it, others dismiss it as mechanical and unmusical. The truth, as always, is somewhere in between. Practiced correctly, Hanon can build extraordinary finger technique. Practiced poorly, it can build tension and bad habits.
## Who Was Charles-Louis Hanon?
Charles-Louis Hanon (1819–1900) was a French piano teacher and composer who believed that technical facility could be systematized. His "Virtuoso Pianist" was designed as a complete [method](/blog/best-piano-method-books-compared): Part 1 (exercises 1–20) builds elementary finger independence, Part 2 (21–40) develops scale and arpeggio technique, and Part 3 (41–60) tackles advanced virtuoso patterns.
Hanon's philosophy was that the fingers should be trained independently and equally. His exercises are designed to strengthen each finger — particularly the naturally weak 4th and 5th fingers — through repetitive patterns that isolate specific movements.
The historical context is important. Hanon wrote in an era when piano technique was rapidly advancing, and pianists were pushing the limits of what was physically possible on the instrument. His exercises were a response to the demand for systematic technical training.
## Exercise 1–20: The Foundation
Exercise No. 1 is the most famous. The pattern is: C-D-E-F-G-F-E-D-C, then shift up one note and repeat. This creates a wave that trains every finger in both ascending and descending motion. The key is absolute evenness — no note should be louder or faster than any other.
The exercises progress in difficulty. No. 1 uses all five fingers in sequence. Later exercises introduce intervals, finger substitutions, and more complex patterns. Each exercise targets a specific technical challenge: stretching, crossing under the thumb, repeated notes, trills.
Proper technique for Hanon No. 1: keep your fingers curved and close to the keys. Don't lift fingers high between notes — small, efficient movements are the goal. Keep your wrist relaxed and flexible. Use a metronome at quarter note = 60 and play one note per beat. Only increase the tempo when you can play with perfect evenness.
## How to Practice Hanon Effectively
**Slow and deliberate** is the golden rule. Hanon practiced fast builds speed without control. Hanon practiced slowly builds control that enables speed. Start at a tempo where you can monitor every aspect of your playing — finger position, wrist tension, tone quality, evenness.
**All keys, not just C major.** This is the most common mistake. Playing Hanon only in C major trains your fingers in one position. Transposing to all 12 keys builds real keyboard knowledge and ensures that every finger works in every position. Start with easy keys (G, F, D) and gradually add sharps and flats.
**Variations and transpositions** keep Hanon interesting and challenging. Try playing in different rhythms (dotted, triplets, syncopated), different articulations (staccato, legato, portato), and different dynamics (crescendo ascending, diminuendo descending). These variations transform a mechanical exercise into a musical one.
## [Common Mistakes](/blog/piano-ornamentation-and-trills-guide) to Avoid
**Tension and speed** are the twin enemies of effective Hanon practice. If your wrists, arms, or shoulders feel tight, you're playing too fast or too forcefully. Stop, shake out your hands, and return at a slower tempo with lighter touch. Hanon should never cause pain.
**Mindless repetition** is the other danger. Playing Hanon while thinking about something else reinforces whatever habits you have — good or bad. Stay present. Listen to every note. Monitor your body. If your mind wanders, stop and refocus.
**Ignoring musicality** turns Hanon into a chore. Even though the exercises aren't "music" in the traditional sense, you can play them musically — with phrasing, dynamics, and rhythmic nuance. This makes practice more enjoyable and builds the habit of playing musically in all contexts.
## [Beyond](/blog/classical-piano-repertoire-by-era-guide) Hanon: Complementary Exercises
Hanon is not the only finger exercise system, and it shouldn't be the only one you use. **Czerny's** studies (particularly [Op. 599](/blog/czerny-piano-exercises-for-technique) and Op. 849) are more musical than Hanon and develop technique in the context of actual pieces. **Cramer's** études are more advanced and bridge the gap between exercises and repertoire.
**Modern alternatives** include Pischna exercises, which are more compact than Hanon and equally effective, and Dohnanyi's "Essential Finger Exercises," which are more challenging and comprehensive. Many modern teachers prefer these to Hanon for their musicality and efficiency.
**[Building](/blog/piano-repertoire-for-small-hands) your own routine** is the ultimate goal. A balanced technical warm-up might include: five minutes of Hanon (for finger independence), five minutes of Czerny (for musical technique), and five minutes of scales and arpeggios (for keyboard navigation). This combination is more effective than any single system alone.
Remember: exercises are tools, not destinations. The goal of Hanon is not to play Hanon well — it's to play everything else well. Use exercises to build the technique you need, then spend the majority of your practice time on actual music. That's where the real joy lives.
