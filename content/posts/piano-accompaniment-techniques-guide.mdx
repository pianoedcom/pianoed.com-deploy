Playing the piano alone is one pleasure; playing with someone else is another entirely. Accompaniment — supporting a singer, instrumentalist, or choir — is one of the most important and rewarding skills a pianist can develop. It asks you to listen more than you play, to lead by following, and to make the other person sound their best. Great accompanists are among the most valued musicians in the world.
This guide covers the core techniques of piano accompaniment, with warmth for beginners and depth for experienced players.
## The Mindset of an Accompanist
The first thing to understand is that accompaniment is a different art from solo playing. Your job is not to shine but to support. This requires:
- **Active listening**: You must hear the soloist's phrasing, breath, and intentions, often before they happen.
- **Flexibility**: Tempo and dynamics may shift; you must follow instantly.
- **Restraint**: The accompaniment should never overpower the melody.
- **Anticipation**: You learn to sense where the soloist is going and arrive together.
A great accompanist makes the soloist feel held — supported, free, and confident.
<Callout title="Accompanist vs. collaborator — a shift in language">
Today, many musicians prefer the term *collaborative pianist* to *accompanist*, reflecting the equal artistic partnership involved. The piano part in Schubert's songs, for instance, is as expressive as the vocal line.
</Callout>
## Core Accompaniment Techniques
### Balance and Voicing
The soloist must always be heard. This means:
- **Keeping the accompaniment quieter** than the melody, even when the music grows.
- **Voicing [chords](/blog/how-to-play-piano-by-ear)** so the top note (often the melodic line) sings out.
- **Reducing texture** in dense passages so the soloist is not buried.
### Following Breath and Phrasing
With singers, you must learn to follow the breath. A singer will naturally slow at the end of a phrase; you must slow with them. Wind players need places to breathe; you must leave space. String players shape phrases with bowing; you must match their ebb and flow.
### Rhythmic Flexibility
Accompaniment often requires *rubato* — a flexible sense of time. The key is to follow the soloist's pulse without losing the underlying beat. Practice with a metronome to develop steady rhythm, then learn to bend it in service of the music.
<Tip>Record yourself accompanying a singer. When you listen back, can you always hear the voice clearly? If not, your accompaniment is too loud — lighten your touch.</Tip>
### Sight Reading and Score Study
Accompanists are often asked to play on short notice, so strong sight-reading skills are essential. Beyond reading the notes, learn to study the full score: understand the structure, the harmonic shape, and where the soloist's difficulties lie. This lets you anticipate problems and support the [performance](/blog/how-to-memorize-piano-pieces-faster).
## Accompanying Different Soloists
### Singers
Vocal accompaniment is its own art. You must:
- **Support the breath**: Match the singer's phrasing and give them room to breathe.
- **Understand the text**: The piano part often illustrates the words — a storm, a heartbeat, a sigh.
- **Handle recitative**: In opera and oratorio, follow the singer's free rhythm closely.
For more on this, see our guide on [how to accompany yourself singing on piano](/blog/how-to-accompany-yourself-singing-on-piano).
### Instrumentalists
With instrumentalists, the partnership is more equal. You must:
- **Match articulation**: If the violinist plays detached, mirror it in the piano.
- **Balance the texture**: In sonata [repertoire](/blog/how-to-build-piano-finger-independence), the piano often has melodic material too.
- **Coordinate entries**: Practice starts and stops together until they feel like one thought.
### Choirs and Ensembles
Choral accompaniment requires a firm, supportive sound. You must:
- **Provide harmonic support** without covering the voices.
- **Follow the conductor** precisely, since tempo may differ from what feels natural.
- **Reinforce difficult passages** by playing parts subtly when singers need help.
## [Common Pitfalls](/blog/piano-bass-patterns-for-accompaniment)
- **Playing too loud**: The most common mistake. Always err on the side of softer.
- **Rushing or dragging**: A soloist who slows must be followed, not pushed.
- **Ignoring the text**: In vocal music, the words shape the music.
- **Over-pedaling**: A blurred accompaniment obscures the soloist's diction.
For more on shaping phrases expressively, see our guide on [piano phrasing techniques](/blog/piano-phrasing-techniques-guide).
## The Art of Support
Accompaniment is a humble art, but it is not a small one. The best accompanists are musicians of extraordinary sensitivity, and their work makes possible the performances audiences cherish. Whether you are playing for a friend's recital, accompanying a choir, or just singing with yourself at the piano, the skills of accompaniment will make you a more complete, more listening musician. In the end, music is about connection — and accompaniment is connection made audible.
