Reharmonization is the art of taking an existing chord progression and replacing or enriching its chords to create a new harmonic landscape. It is one of the most creative and expressive skills a pianist can develop. A simple folk song can become a jazz ballad; a familiar standard can sound fresh and surprising; a plain melody can take on emotional depth it never had before. Reharmonization is central to jazz, where players constantly reinvent the harmony of standards, and it is increasingly used in pop, film, and contemporary classical music to add sophistication. Far from mere decoration, reharmonization is a form of musical storytelling — it lets you reinterpret a piece and make it your own.
This guide introduces the core techniques of reharmonization, from simple substitutions to advanced harmonic devices, and shows you how to apply them to transform progressions.
## The Purpose of Reharmonization
Before learning techniques, it helps to understand why we reharmonize. Reharmonization serves several purposes. It can add color and interest to a progression that feels too plain. It can highlight or support a melody note by giving it a fresh harmonic context. It can create contrast between sections, making a return to the original harmony feel like a homecoming. And it can express a personal harmonic voice, turning a standard into something uniquely yours.
The guiding [principle](/blog/piano-comping-techniques-for-jazz) is always to serve the music. Reharmonization should enhance the melody and the emotional arc, not overshadow them. The best reharmonizations feel inevitable, as if the new harmony were always meant to be there.
## Basic Reharmonization Techniques
Start with these foundational techniques, which can be applied to almost any progression.
### Chord Substitution
The simplest reharmonization is to replace a chord with a related one. As covered in the [chord substitutions guide](/blog/piano-chord-substitutions-guide), you can replace a tonic chord with its relative minor, a subdominant with a ii chord, or a dominant with its tritone substitute. These substitutions preserve the harmonic function while adding color. Start by substituting one or two chords in a simple progression and listen to the effect.
### Adding Passing Chords
Insert chords between existing ones to create smoother or more interesting motion. A common device is the passing diminished chord: between Cmaj7 and Dm7, insert C♯dim7, creating a chromatic [bass line](/blog/bossa-nova-piano-patterns-tutorial) C–C♯–D. Passing chords add motion and sophistication without changing the destination. Another common passing chord is the secondary dominant — a dominant chord that resolves to a chord other than the tonic, such as A7 resolving to Dm7 in the key of C.
### Extending the Harmony
Add color tones to existing chords rather than replacing them. Turn a plain C major triad into Cmaj7, Cmaj9, or Cmaj13. Add altered tensions to dominant chords — flat ninths, sharp elevenths. These extensions enrich the harmony without changing its function, and they are the simplest way to make a progression sound more modern and colorful.
## [Intermediate](/blog/jazz-piano-voicings-for-intermediate-players) Techniques
These techniques add more significant harmonic change.
### Modal Interchange
Modal interchange (or borrowed chords) involves borrowing chords from a parallel mode — typically the parallel minor. In C major, you might borrow the minor iv chord (Fm) from C minor, or the minor-six chord (A♭maj7), or the half-diminished ii (Dm7♭5). These borrowed chords add a bittersweet, sophisticated color while still resolving naturally within the key. Modal interchange is a staple of jazz and ballad harmony.
### Reharmonizing with Bass Motion
Change the bass note under a melody to create a new harmony. If the melody note is E over a C chord, you might reharmonize by [putting](/blog/blues-piano-lessons-for-beginners) A in the bass (creating Am7), or F (creating Fmaj7), or even E♭ (creating a more colorful chord). This technique lets you keep the melody while completely changing its harmonic context. It is one of the most powerful reharmonization tools because it preserves the melody — the most important voice — while transforming everything beneath it.
### Approach Chords
Add a chord a half step above or below a target chord, creating a chromatic approach. Before a Cmaj7, play D♭maj7, then resolve down to C. Or play Bmaj7, resolving up to C. These chromatic approaches add tension and a sense of arrival. They are common in jazz and can make even a simple progression sound sophisticated.
## Advanced Techniques
For pianists ready to push further, these techniques create more dramatic reharmonization.
### Contrapuntal Reharmonization
Reharmonize based on the melodic line, treating the melody as a voice in counterpoint and finding harmonies that complement it. This approach can lead to surprising, non-functional harmonies that still support the melody beautifully. It requires thinking less in terms of chord progressions and more in terms of lines moving against each other.
### Complete Harmonic Overhaul
In the most radical reharmonization, you replace the entire progression with a new one that still fits the melody. This is common in jazz, where a standard's original changes may be entirely replaced with a new set. This requires deep harmonic knowledge and a strong ear, but it can produce striking, original results. Study how pianists like Bill Evans and Herbie Hancock completely reharmonize standards to hear this in action.
<Callout type="tip" title="Reharmonize One Tune Many Ways">
Take a simple tune — a folk song or a standard — and reharmonize it in several different ways: one version with chord substitutions, one with passing chords, one with modal interchange, one with changed bass motion. Comparing your versions teaches you how each technique shapes the music's character. This exercise builds both your harmonic vocabulary and your ear for what each reharmonization accomplishes.
</Callout>
## Practical Application
Reharmonization is a skill built through practice and experimentation.
### Start with Simple Tunes
Begin with simple, familiar tunes — folk songs, nursery rhymes, or easy standards. Their simple harmony gives you room to experiment. Apply one technique at a time, listening carefully to the effect. Gradually combine techniques as you become comfortable with each.
### Preserve the Melody
Always keep the melody as your anchor. A reharmonization that clashes with the melody will sound wrong, no matter how clever the harmony. Sing or play the melody as you experiment with harmonies, and let the melody guide your choices.
### Trust Your Ear
Theory gives you options, but the ear is the final judge. Some reharmonizations that look right on paper sound awkward, and some that seem unusual sound beautiful. Trust your ear above the theory. If it sounds good, it is good.
## Common Pitfalls
A few cautions will keep your reharmonizations musical.
### Over-Reharmonizing
The temptation, once you know these techniques, is to reharmonize every chord. But too much reharmonization can make a progression lose its shape and direction, and it can overshadow the melody. Restraint is key — reharmonize selectively, highlighting key moments and leaving others plain for contrast.
### Losing the Form
A reharmonization should still respect the form of the tune. The listener should still sense the [phrase](/blog/jazz-piano-improvisation-basics-guide) structure and the harmonic rhythm. If your reharmonization obscures the form, the music can feel lost. Keep the large-scale shape clear even as you enrich the details.
## [Conclusion](/blog/piano-walking-bass-lines-guide)
Reharmonization is one of the most creative and satisfying skills in a pianist's toolkit. It lets you reinvent music, express your harmonic voice, and find new depth in familiar melodies. Start with the basic techniques — substitution, passing chords, and color tones — and gradually explore modal interchange, bass reharmonization, and beyond. Apply them to simple tunes, preserve the melody, and trust your ear. With practice, reharmonization becomes a natural part of your musical thinking, and you will find yourself transforming progressions spontaneously, creating harmonies that are both sophisticated and deeply personal. It is the art of hearing anew, and it opens a lifetime of harmonic exploration.
