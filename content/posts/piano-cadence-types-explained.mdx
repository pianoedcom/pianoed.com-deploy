Music needs punctuation just as speech does. A cadence is a chord progression that creates a point of resolution or arrival — the musical equivalent of a comma, period, or question mark. Understanding cadences helps you understand how phrases end, how tension is built and released, and how to interpret the shape of the music you play. For [pianists](/blog/piano-counterpoint-basics-guide), recognizing cadences also makes sight-reading and memorization easier, because you start to hear where the music is going.
Cadences are built from chords, so a little chord knowledge helps. But the concepts themselves are simple and immediately useful.
## What Is a Cadence?
A cadence is a two-chord progression at the end of a phrase. The specific chords and their order determine the type of cadence and the feeling it creates — finality, suspension, surprise, or continuation.
<Callout type="note">
Think of a cadence as the punctuation at the end of a musical sentence. The same words can end with a period (final), a comma (pause), or a question mark (open) — cadences do the same for music.
</Callout>
We'll use Roman numerals in the key of C major: I (C), IV (F), V (G), vi (Am).
## The Four Main Cadence Types
### 1. Authentic Cadence (V–I)
The strongest, most final-sounding cadence. It moves from the dominant (V) to the tonic (I), creating a sense of completion.
- **Perfect authentic cadence (PAC)** — both chords in root [position](/blog/piano-glissando-technique-guide), the melody ends on the tonic. Maximum finality. "The end."
- **Imperfect authentic cadence** — one or both chords inverted, or melody ends on a non-tonic note. Still conclusive, but softer.
### 2. Plagal Cadence (IV–I)
The "amen" cadence, common in hymns. It moves from the subdominant (IV) to the tonic (I). Gentler and less driving than the authentic, it feels warm and settled rather than emphatic.
### 3. Half Cadence (any chord–V)
A cadence that ends on the dominant (V) rather than resolving to tonic. It creates a sense of suspension or question — "to be continued." The phrase feels incomplete and expects a follow-up.
### 4. Deceptive Cadence (V–vi)
The V chord resolves somewhere other than the expected I — usually to vi. It's a surprise: the music sets up a perfect ending then swerves, prolonging the phrase and creating a richer, longer structure.
## Hearing the Cadences
The best way to internalize cadences is to play and listen to each:
1. Play G–C (V–I in C) — notice the sense of arrival. That's an authentic cadence.
2. Play F–C (IV–I) — notice the gentler, "amen" warmth. That's plagal.
3. Play C–G (I–V) — notice the open, unfinished feeling. That's a half cadence.
4. Play G–Am (V–vi) — notice the surprise. That's deceptive.
<Callout type="tip">
Play each cadence several times and sing or hum the final note. Your ear will start to recognize the characteristic feeling of each type within minutes.
</Callout>
## Why Cadences Matter for Pianists
- **Phrasing** — knowing where cadences fall tells you where to breathe, lift, and shape a phrase
- **Memorization** — music becomes easier to remember when you understand its structural points
- **Sight-reading** — you anticipate where a phrase is heading, reading ahead more confidently
- **Improvisation and composition** — cadences are the tools for shaping your own musical sentences
- **Interpretation** — a deceptive cadence invites a subtle rubato; a perfect cadence wants clarity
## Cadences in Context
In a typical [classical](/blog/how-to-trill-on-piano-with-control) phrase, you'll often find a half cadence in the middle (opening the phrase) and an authentic cadence at the end (closing it). Longer pieces chain many cadences together, using deceptive and half cadences to extend phrases before the final authentic resolution.
When you learn a piece, mark the cadences in your score. You'll quickly see the architecture — where the composer pauses, prolongs, and resolves — and your interpretation will deepen.
## [Practice Plan](/blog/how-to-play-piano-tremolo-technique)
1. **Play each cadence type** in C major until you recognize its character
2. **Identify cadences in a simple piece** — mark them and listen for the feeling
3. **Transpose to another key** — play the same cadences in G or F
4. **Improvise short phrases** ending with each cadence type
Within a week of attention, you'll start hearing cadences everywhere — and your playing will reflect a new understanding of musical structure.
## Common Confusions
- **Half vs authentic** — half ends on V (open); authentic ends on I (closed)
- **Plagal vs authentic** — plagal uses IV; authentic uses V. Both end on I, but plagal feels gentler
- **Deceptive vs half** — deceptive resolves V somewhere unexpected; half simply stops on V
Cadences are the grammar of musical phrasing. Learn the four main types, play them until their characters are familiar, and you'll hear music with new structural ears. It's a small investment of understanding that pays off in every piece you ever play.
