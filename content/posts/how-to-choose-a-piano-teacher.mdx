The decision to study with a piano teacher is one of the most important choices you'll make in your piano journey. A good teacher can accelerate your progress, prevent you from developing bad habits, open your ears to new music, and provide the accountability and encouragement that keep you motivated. A poor teacher can do the opposite — reinforcing bad technique, assigning inappropriate repertoire, or draining your enthusiasm for the instrument. The difference between a teacher who transforms your playing and one who holds you back is enormous.
This guide will help you navigate the process of finding, evaluating, and choosing a piano teacher. Whether you're an adult beginner looking for your first teacher or an intermediate pianist seeking to take the next step, the principles are the same: know what you need, know what to look for, and ask the right questions. The right teacher is out there — you just need to know how to find them.
## Do You Need a Teacher?
The benefits of guided learning are substantial. A teacher provides something that no [method](/blog/best-piano-books-for-self-taught-adults) book, app, or YouTube tutorial can: real-time feedback. When you play a passage with incorrect fingering, uneven rhythm, or tense posture, a teacher can identify the problem immediately and guide you to a solution. This immediate feedback loop prevents you from practicing mistakes — which is the single most common reason pianists develop bad habits that take years to unlearn.
A teacher also provides structure and progression. Without guidance, self-taught pianists often plateau because they don't know what to work on next. They practice the same pieces, the same exercises, the same techniques, without the systematic progression that leads to growth. A teacher assesses your current level, identifies the next steps, and assigns material that challenges you appropriately — not too easy (boring) and not too hard (frustrating).
When self-teaching works well is for pianists who are highly self-motivated, who have clear goals, and who have access to quality [learning resources](/blog/how-to-learn-piano-as-an-adult). If you're disciplined about practicing regularly, if you can assess your own playing objectively (perhaps by recording yourself), and if you have good method books and online resources, you can make significant progress on your own. Many adult beginners start with self-teaching and add a teacher when they hit a plateau or want to tackle more advanced repertoire.
Hybrid approaches combine the best of both worlds. You might take lessons monthly (rather than weekly) for guidance and accountability, while working through method books on your own in between. Or you might take a series of lessons to establish good technique and then continue independently. Some pianists take occasional "check-up" lessons — a single session every few months to get feedback and course correction. The key is to find the arrangement that fits your [schedule](/blog/piano-practice-routine-for-busy-adults), budget, and learning style.
<Callout>
**The feedback gap**: The biggest challenge of self-teaching is the lack of feedback. You can't hear yourself play the way others hear you — your brain filters out the imperfections. Recording yourself is the best substitute for a teacher's ear, but it requires discipline and honest self-assessment. If you find you're not making progress on your own, a teacher can help you identify why.
</Callout>
## What to Look For in a Teacher
Qualifications and experience [matter](/blog/best-piano-finger-exercises-for-beginners), but they're not the only factors. A teacher with a music degree from a conservatory has demonstrated a high level of musical training, but that doesn't automatically make them a good teacher. Teaching is a skill separate from performing — the ability to play beautifully doesn't guarantee the ability to help others learn to play. Look for qualifications that specifically relate to teaching: a pedagogy certificate, experience teaching students at your level, or membership in professional organizations like the Music Teachers National Association (MTNA).
Teaching style and personality fit are arguably more important than credentials. Every teacher has a different approach: some are strict and demanding, others are warm and encouraging. Some emphasize technique and discipline, others emphasize musicality and enjoyment. Some follow a structured curriculum, others adapt to each student's interests. The best teacher for you is one whose style matches your learning preferences and whose personality you respond well to. A brilliant teacher whose style clashes with yours will be less effective than a competent teacher whose style fits you perfectly.
Specialization is worth considering. If your goal is to play classical piano, a teacher who specializes in classical training will serve you better than a jazz specialist. If you want to play jazz, look for a teacher with jazz performance experience. If you're interested in pop piano and accompanying [yourself singing](/blog/how-to-accompany-yourself-singing-on-piano), find a teacher who plays and teaches in that style. Many teachers are versatile across genres, but most have areas of particular strength. Ask about their specialties and make sure they align with your goals.
Experience teaching adults is a specific qualification worth seeking if you're an adult learner. Teaching adults is different from teaching children — adults have different motivations, different learning styles, and different challenges (less flexible fingers, more self-conscious, busier schedules). A teacher who has experience with adult students will understand these differences and adapt their approach accordingly. Ask potential teachers about their experience teaching adults and their approach to adult learners.
<Note>
**The trial lesson**: Most teachers offer a trial lesson — either free or at a reduced rate. Take advantage of this. A single lesson will tell you more about whether a teacher is right for you than any amount of research. Pay attention to how the teacher communicates, whether you feel comfortable, and whether their approach makes sense to you.
</Note>
## Where to Find Piano Teachers
Local music schools and conservatories are traditional sources of piano teachers. Community music schools (often affiliated with universities or conservatories) offer lessons with trained teachers at various price points. These schools typically vet their teachers, provide a structured curriculum, and offer recital opportunities. The quality is generally high, and the institutional setting provides accountability and professionalism.
Online directories and platforms have become popular ways to find teachers. Websites like Lessons.com, Thumbtack, and TakeLessons allow you to search for piano teachers in your area, read reviews, and compare prices. These platforms are convenient, but the quality of teachers varies widely — some are excellent, others are less qualified. Use the directory to find candidates, but do your own due diligence: check their qualifications, ask for references, and schedule a trial lesson.
Community recommendations are often the most reliable source. Ask friends, family, coworkers, or local music stores for recommendations. If you know someone who plays piano and whose playing you admire, ask who their teacher is. Personal recommendations come with the endorsement of someone whose judgment you trust, which is more valuable than an anonymous online review. Local piano teachers often have networks of colleagues they can recommend if they're not accepting new students.
Online piano lessons have become a viable option, especially since 2020. Platforms like Lessonface, Musika, and TakeLessons offer video lessons with teachers around the world. Online lessons have some disadvantages (the teacher can't physically adjust your hand position, and audio quality can affect the teacher's ability to hear nuances), but they also have advantages: you have access to a much larger pool of teachers, you can study with specialists who don't live in your area, and you save travel time. For many adult learners, online lessons are a practical and effective option.
## Questions to Ask Before Committing
Trial lessons and expectations: ask whether the teacher offers a trial lesson (most do) and what their expectations are for new students. Do they expect you to practice a certain amount per week? Do they assign specific method books? Do they have recitals or performance requirements? Understanding expectations upfront prevents misunderstandings later.
Lesson structure and curriculum: ask how the teacher structures lessons. A typical lesson might include: review of the previous week's assignment, technique work (scales, exercises), repertoire work (pieces you're learning), and assignment of new material. Ask what method books or materials they use, and whether they adapt their curriculum to each student's interests. A teacher who insists on a rigid curriculum without considering your goals may not be the best fit.
Cancellation and make-up policies are practical considerations that affect your experience. Most teachers have policies for missed lessons — some offer make-up lessons, others don't. Ask about their policy before committing, and make sure it works with your schedule. If you travel frequently for work or have an unpredictable schedule, a teacher with a strict no-make-up policy may not be practical.
Payment and scheduling logistics: ask about the teacher's rate (typically $30–$80 per hour, varying by location and experience), whether they bill monthly or per lesson, and what payment methods they accept. Ask about lesson length (30, 45, or 60 minutes — adults usually benefit from 45–60 minutes) and frequency (weekly is standard, but biweekly can work for some). Understanding these logistics upfront ensures there are no surprises.
## Making the Most of Your Lessons
Preparing for each lesson maximizes the value of your time with the teacher. Practice the assigned material consistently during the week. Note any difficulties you encountered — specific passages that were hard, concepts that were confusing, questions that came up. Bring these notes to the lesson so you can address them directly. A teacher can't help you with problems they don't know about. The more specific you can be about what you're struggling with, the more effectively the teacher can help.
Practicing between sessions is where the real learning happens. The lesson is guidance; the practice is implementation. A teacher can show you what to do, but you have to do it — repeatedly, consistently, with attention — for it to become part of your playing. Aim to practice at least 4–5 days per week, for at least 20–30 minutes per session. Less than that, and you'll spend most of each lesson relearning what you've forgotten since the last one.
When to switch teachers is a sensitive topic, but an important one. Not every teacher-student relationship works out, and that's okay. Signs that it might be time to switch include: you've been studying for months but feel you're not making progress; the teacher's style consistently clashes with your learning preferences; you've outgrown the teacher's expertise (you're now more advanced than they can accommodate); or you've lost enthusiasm for piano since starting lessons. Changing teachers can feel awkward, but your musical development is what matters. A good teacher will understand if you need to move on, and may even recommend a colleague who's a better fit.
The right teacher is a partner in your musical journey — someone who guides, challenges, encourages, and inspires you. Finding that person takes effort, but the reward is immeasurable. When you find a teacher who helps you play better than you thought you could, who opens your ears to music you'd never discovered, and who makes you look forward to every lesson, you'll know the search was worth it. That teacher is out there. Start looking.
