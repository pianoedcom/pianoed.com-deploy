One of the most common questions pianists ask — at every level — is "What should I play next?" Choosing repertoire is both exciting and daunting. Pick something too easy and you'll be bored; pick something too hard and you'll be frustrated. The ideal piece is one that challenges you in specific ways while remaining within your overall technical grasp — a piece that stretches your abilities without breaking them.
This guide maps out a progressive repertoire path from first-year beginner to advanced pianist. It's organized by skill level, with specific piece recommendations at each stage. The goal is to help you build a repertoire that develops your technique systematically, exposes you to a range of musical styles, and keeps you engaged and motivated. Think of it as a curriculum — but one you can adapt to your own interests and goals.
## Beginner (First Year): [Building](/blog/piano-jazz-standard-repertoire-list) Foundations
In your first year of piano, the focus should be on building foundational skills: reading notation, coordinating the two hands, developing basic finger independence, and understanding simple rhythmic patterns. The repertoire at this stage should be simple enough that you can focus on these fundamentals without being overwhelmed by technical demands.
[Simple melodies](/blog/piano-melody-ornamentation-techniques) and two-hand coordination are the core skills at this stage. Pieces like "Ode to Joy" from Beethoven's Ninth Symphony, "When the Saints Go Marching In," and "Amazing Grace" introduce you to basic melody playing in the right hand with simple chord or single-note accompaniment in the left hand. These pieces teach you to read notation, count rhythms, and coordinate your hands — the [essential](/blog/jazz-piano-sheet-music-recommendations) skills that everything else builds upon.
Bach's Minuet in G Major (BWV Anh. 114) is a classic first-year piece that introduces two-voice texture — each hand plays an independent melody line. It's more challenging than a single-melody piece but still accessible to beginners, and it introduces the contrapuntal thinking that Bach's music requires. The Clementi Sonatina in C Major, Op. 36 No. 1, is another first-year milestone — it introduces the sonatina form (a simplified sonata with multiple movements) and requires more sophisticated hand coordination.
Repertoire that teaches basic skills at this level includes method book pieces (from Faber, Alfred, or Bastien), simplified arrangements of familiar tunes, and easy [classical pieces](/blog/classical-piano-sheet-music-for-beginners). The key is to choose pieces that introduce one new challenge at a time — a new note, a new rhythm, a new hand position — while reinforcing the skills you've already developed. Don't rush through this stage. The foundations you build in your first year will support everything you play for the rest of your life.
<Callout>
**Practice tip for beginners**: Choose pieces that you can learn in 1–2 weeks of regular practice. This gives you the satisfaction of completing pieces regularly, which maintains motivation. Pieces that take months to learn can be discouraging at this stage — save the longer projects for when your skills are more developed.
</Callout>
## Early Intermediate (Years 2–3)
In your second and third years, you're ready for more complex repertoire. You can read notation fluently, coordinate your hands, and play with some dynamic control. The focus shifts to developing expression, building speed, and tackling more sophisticated musical forms.
Introducing classical forms and expression means studying pieces that have structural complexity — sonatinas, theme and variations, and simple sonata movements. Burgmüller's Op. 100, "25 Progressive Studies," is an ideal collection for this level. Each study is a short character piece that focuses on a specific technical and expressive challenge: arpeggios, broken chords, [articulation](/blog/piano-articulation-marks-guide), phrasing. They're musical enough to be satisfying and progressive enough to build technique systematically.
Selected Chopin Preludes are accessible at this level. The Prelude in E Minor, Op. 28 No. 4, is a beautiful, emotionally powerful piece that's technically approachable — it's slow, the left hand plays simple chords, and the right hand plays a melodic line that requires expressive phrasing but not great technical virtuosity. The Prelude in A Major, Op. 28 No. 7, is another excellent choice — a short, lyrical piece that teaches legato playing and dynamic control. The Prelude in C Major, Op. 28 No. 1, is more technically demanding, with broken chord patterns that build arpeggio technique.
Building speed and independence becomes a focus at this level. Czerny's Op. 599 exercises (see our [Czerny practice guide](/articles/czerny-piano-exercises-for-technique)) provide systematic technical development, and repertoire that applies these skills includes Bach's Two-Part Inventions (start with No. 1 in C Major and No. 4 in D Minor), which develop independence of the hands and contrapuntal thinking, and easier movements from Beethoven's Sonatina in G Major (Anh. 5 No. 1), which introduce classical sonata form.
## Intermediate (Years 3–5)
At the intermediate level, you're ready for substantial repertoire. You have solid technique, can play expressively, and can handle pieces of significant length and complexity. The focus shifts to developing interpretive skills — making musical decisions about tempo, dynamics, phrasing, and character — and tackling larger forms.
Romantic repertoire and larger forms become central at this level. Beethoven's Sonata in G Major, Op. 49 No. 2 (often called the "Easy Sonata") is an excellent first Beethoven sonata — it's technically approachable but musically substantial, with two movements that introduce classical sonata form and Beethoven's characteristic dynamic contrasts. The Sonata in C Minor, Op. 27 No. 2 ("Moonlight" — first movement only) is more challenging but accessible to intermediate pianists, with its famous arpeggio patterns.
[Debussy](/blog/piano-duet-repertoire-for-four-hands)'s "Arabesque No. 1" is a gateway to impressionist piano music. It introduces the flowing, arpeggiated textures and non-functional harmony that characterize Debussy's style, while remaining technically approachable. The "Girl with the Flaxen Hair" and "Clair de Lune" (simplified versions if necessary) are other Debussy options at this level. Chopin's Nocturne in E Flat Major, Op. 9 No. 2, is a quintessential romantic piece that develops expressive playing, rubato, and ornamental technique.
Developing interpretive skills is the key focus at this level. You're no longer just learning the notes — you're making decisions about how to play them. What tempo feels right? Where should the dynamics swell and subside? How should the phrases be shaped? Where should the music breathe? These questions don't have single right answers — they require you to develop your own musical judgment, informed by your understanding of the style, the composer, and the historical context.
## Advanced (5+ Years)
Advanced pianists are ready for the major works of the piano repertoire. This is the level where you tackle the pieces that define the instrument — the sonatas, concertos, and études that represent the pinnacle of piano composition. The focus at this level is on performance preparation, interpretive depth, and technical mastery.
Major works and virtuosic repertoire at this level include Chopin's Ballades (particularly No. 1 in G Minor, which is a watershed piece for advanced pianists), Beethoven's major sonatas (the "Pathétique," "Moonlight" complete, "Waldstein," "Appassionata"), Liszt's Hungarian Rhapsodies and Études, Rachmaninoff's Preludes and Études-Tableaux, and Debussy's larger works like "Estampes" and "Images." These pieces demand not only technical mastery but also deep musical understanding and emotional commitment.
Performance preparation becomes a focus at this level. Advanced pieces are long, complex, and demanding — they require not just learning the notes but internalizing them so thoroughly that you can perform them under pressure. This means practicing performance runs (playing through the entire piece without stopping), recording yourself, playing for others, and developing the mental and physical stamina that performance requires. The gap between "can play in the practice room" and "can perform on stage" is significant, and bridging it is part of the advanced pianist's journey.
## Cross-Genre Repertoire Suggestions
While classical repertoire forms the backbone of most pianists' development, exploring other genres enriches your musicianship and keeps your practice engaging. Jazz standards for each level include: beginner ("Autumn Leaves" with simple chord voicings), intermediate ("Fly Me to the Moon" with walking bass and chord voicings), and advanced ("Take Five" with its distinctive 5/4 rhythm and improvisation sections).
Pop and film arrangements are available at every level. Simplified arrangements of popular songs, film themes, and show tunes can provide a refreshing break from classical repertoire and develop your ear for different musical styles. Many publishers offer "easy piano" versions of popular music that are accessible to beginners and intermediate players.
[Building a balanced repertoire](/blog/classical-piano-repertoire-by-era-guide) means including pieces from different eras, styles, and genres. A well-rounded pianist can play Bach and Beatles, Chopin and Coldplay, Beethoven and Bill Evans. Each genre develops different skills — classical develops technique and reading, jazz develops ear training and improvisation, pop develops chord playing and accompaniment skills. Don't limit yourself to one genre. The more diverse your repertoire, the more versatile and well-rounded a musician you'll become.
Remember that this guide is a starting point, not a prescription. The most important factor in choosing repertoire is your own interest and motivation. If a piece excites you, you'll practice it more diligently and learn it more deeply. If a piece bores you, no amount of pedagogical justification will make you want to play it. Use this guide as a framework, but follow your musical heart — it will rarely lead you wrong.
