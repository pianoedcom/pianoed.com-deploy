Every note on a piano depends on a tiny piece of steel and a block of wood you almost never see. The tuning pins, driven into the pinblock, hold the tremendous tension of each string and allow a tuner to adjust its pitch. When this system is healthy, a piano stays in tune and responds to careful adjustment. When it fails, no amount of tuning skill can keep the piano stable. Understanding tuning pins and the pinblock explains why pianos go out of tune — and when a tuning problem is really a structural one.
## The Role of Tuning Pins
Each piano string is wound around a tuning pin at one end. The pin is a steel cylinder, roughly the thickness of a pencil, with a squared top for the tuning lever (the "tuning hammer") and a hole through which the string passes. The pin is driven tightly into the pinblock, and friction between the pin and the wood holds the string at its tension.
The pin must hold against roughly 150–200 pounds of tension per string — and a piano has over 200 strings, totaling 18–20 tons of combined tension. The pinblock's job is to grip every pin firmly enough that the tension doesn't slowly pull the pin loose.
<Callout type="note">
When a tuner "sets the pin," they're not just moving the pin to the right pitch — they're flexing it slightly so it settles into a stable position, balanced against the string's pull. A well-set pin holds its pitch for months; a poorly set pin slips within days.
</Callout>
## The Pinblock: The Piano's Foundation
The pinblock (also called the wrest plank) is a thick laminated block of hardwood into which all the tuning pins are driven. It sits behind the keyboard in an [upright](/blog/piano-action-types-explained-simply) or at the front of a grand's case. Its quality is one of the biggest factors in a piano's long-term tuning stability.
### How the Pinblock Is Made
Pinblocks are built from multiple thin plies of hardwood — often maple, beech, or birch — glued together under pressure. The lamination gives the block strength and dimensional stability, so it resists the constant tension and the [humidity](/blog/piano-maintenance-and-tuning-guide) swings that would warp a single solid piece. The plies are oriented so the grain runs in alternating directions, distributing the gripping force around each pin.
### Why the Pinblock Matters
A good pinblock grips each pin with consistent, firm friction — enough to hold tension, but not so tight that the pin won't turn during tuning. This balance is delicate. If the wood dries out, the holes shrink and the pins loosen. If the block absorbs moisture, it swells and the pins become too tight to tune smoothly. Over decades, the wood's grip weakens, and pins begin to slip.
## Why Pianos Go Out of Tune
Several forces fight to pull a piano away from its tuning:
### Humidity and Temperature
Wood expands with moisture and contracts when dry. As the pinblock swells and shrinks, the pins shift microscopically, and the [strings](/blog/acoustic-piano-soundboard-explained) and soundboard move too. This is the single biggest cause of seasonal tuning drift. A piano in a humid summer and dry winter will go noticeably out of tune with each season even if never played.
### String Tension and Stretch
New strings stretch over time, which is why freshly restrung pianos need frequent tuning for the first year. Even settled strings slowly creep under constant tension.
### Playing
Hard, vigorous playing knocks strings slightly out of alignment and stresses the pins. A heavily played piano needs more frequent tuning than one rarely touched.
### Structural Movement
The piano's frame, soundboard, and pinblock all move subtly over time. As the [soundboard](/blog/acoustic-piano-soundboard-explained) rises and falls with humidity, it changes the downbearing on the strings, shifting their pitch.
## Signs of Pinblock Trouble
Most tuning drift is normal and solved by regular tuning. But some symptoms point to pinblock problems that need a technician:
- **Pins that won't hold** — the tuner brings a string to pitch, and it slips back within days or even minutes
- **Specific notes that constantly go out** — especially in one area of the piano
- **Pins that feel too loose** — the tuning lever turns them with little resistance
- **Pins that feel too tight or jump** — the wood has swollen or the holes are worn unevenly
- **A piano that won't hold a tuning at all** — a sign of widespread pinblock failure
<Callout type="warning">
If a piano won't hold a tuning across much of its range, the pinblock is likely the culprit. No amount of tuning will fix this — it's a structural repair. Have a technician assess whether the block can be treated or needs replacement.
</Callout>
## Pinblock Problems and Repairs
### Loose Pins
When pins loosen, technicians have several options:
- **Pin tightening compounds** — a liquid applied to the pin holes that swells the wood fibers slightly, restoring grip. A common, affordable fix for mild looseness
- **Larger pins** — driving slightly thicker tuning pins into the existing holes for a tighter fit. Effective when the holes are only mildly worn
- **Pin replacement with bushings** — installing thin wood bushings in enlarged holes, then driving standard pins. A more involved repair for moderate wear
- **Pinblock replacement** — replacing the entire block. The most expensive repair, reserved for pianos worth the investment
### Cracked or Separated Pinblock
If the pinblock cracks or separates from the plies, pins in the affected area won't hold. Cracks can sometimes be repaired with epoxy and re-drilling; severe damage requires block replacement.
### Environmental Damage
A pinblock that has been repeatedly soaked or dried out may be irreversibly damaged. [Humidity control](/blog/piano-humidity-control-and-climate) is preventive medicine — it protects the pinblock before problems start.
## Caring for the Pinblock
You can't see the pinblock, but you can protect it:
- **Control humidity** — stable 42–50% relative humidity is the single best protection against pinblock damage
- **Keep the piano away from heat and moisture sources** — radiators, vents, windows, and exterior walls all stress the wood
- **Tune regularly** — regular tuning keeps tension balanced and lets a technician spot developing problems early
- **Don't move pins yourself** — amateur tuning with the wrong lever or technique can damage pins and the block
- **Have a technician assess older pianos** — a 50-year-old piano's pinblock may be nearing the end of its reliable life
## How Often Should a Piano Be Tuned?
For a healthy piano in a stable environment:
- **New pianos** — 3–4 times in the first year as strings settle
- **Home pianos** — at least twice a year, ideally with the changing seasons
- **Heavily played or performance pianos** — before each significant use, or monthly
- **Pianos in unstable environments** — more often, until the environment is controlled
If your piano won't stay in tune between visits despite a stable room, the pinblock may be the issue — ask your technician to check pin tightness.
## The Pinblock and the Piano's Value
A sound pinblock is foundational to a piano's worth. A beautiful case and fine action mean little if the pins won't hold. When evaluating a used piano, the pinblock's condition is one of the most important factors a technician checks — it can be the difference between a great instrument and an expensive repair.
The tuning pins and pinblock are hidden, but they do the piano's hardest work. Keep the environment stable, tune regularly, and respect their role, and they'll keep your piano singing in tune for decades.
