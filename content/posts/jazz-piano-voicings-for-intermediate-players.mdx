If you've been playing jazz piano for a while and can navigate basic seventh chords and [the blues](/blog/jazz-piano-improvisation-basics-guide), you've likely reached a plateau where your playing sounds correct but not quite "jazzy." The missing ingredient is almost certainly voicings — the specific ways chords are arranged and voiced on the keyboard. The difference between a beginner jazz pianist and an intermediate one is largely a matter of voicing [vocabulary](/blog/piano-boogie-woogie-style-guide).
This guide introduces the [essential](/blog/piano-blues-scale-patterns-and-licks) voicings that every intermediate jazz pianist should know, from rootless voicings to drop-2, drop-3, and quartal structures. Each voicing type has its own sound, its own use case, and its own way of making your playing sound more professional and authentic.
## Understanding Jazz Voicings
### Why Voicings Matter More Than Chords in Jazz
In jazz, the chord symbols on a lead sheet are not instructions — they're suggestions. A Cm7 symbol doesn't mean you must play C-Eb-G-Bb in root position. It means the harmony is C minor 7, and you're free to voice that harmony in any way that serves the music. The voicing you choose determines the color, texture, and character of the harmony.
A root position Cm7 played by a beginner sounds clunky and unidiomatic. The same chord voiced as Eb-G-Bb-D (a rootless voicing) sounds warm, professional, and distinctly jazz. The notes are the same — the [arrangement](/blog/piano-arrangement-techniques-for-pop-songs) is different. This is the essence of jazz voicing: it's not what notes you play, it's how you arrange them.
### The Evolution from Basic Triads to Extended Harmony
Jazz harmony evolved from the simple triads and seventh chords of early New Orleans jazz to the extended and altered harmonies of bebop and modern jazz. As the music became more sophisticated, pianists developed voicings that could accommodate 9ths, 11ths, 13ths, and altered tones without sounding muddy or cluttered. These voicings — rootless, drop-2, quartal — are the tools that allow a pianist to play rich, colorful harmony with just two hands.
### Voice Leading [Principles](/blog/piano-walking-bass-lines-guide)
Good voice leading is the secret behind smooth, professional-sounding jazz piano. The principle is simple: when moving from one chord to the next, move each voice by the smallest possible interval. [Common tones](/blog/piano-comping-techniques-for-jazz) should stay put, and other notes should move by step whenever possible. This creates a seamless, connected sound where chords flow into each other rather than jumping from one block to the next.
<Callout>
**The goal of voicing is not to play every note of the chord.** It's to play the notes that define the chord's character — the 3rd, 7th, and color tones (9ths, 11ths, 13ths) — while leaving out the root and 5th, which are less essential and can muddy the sound.
</Callout>
## Rootless Voicings
### What Rootless Voicings Are and Why They're Essential
Rootless voicings are exactly what they sound like — chord voicings that omit the root note. Since the bass player typically covers the root, the pianist doesn't need to play it. This frees up the pianist's hands to focus on the upper extensions of the chord — the 3rd, 7th, 9th, 11th, and 13th — which are the notes that give the chord its color and character.
Rootless voicings were popularized by pianists like Bud Powell, Bill Evans, and Wynton Kelly, and they've become the standard approach for jazz piano comping. They sound more professional than root-position chords, they're easier to play (the hand spans are smaller), and they leave sonic space for the bass player.
### A-Type and B-Type Rootless Voicings
For each chord, there are two common rootless voicings, called A-type and B-type. They contain the same notes but in a different order, providing two different sounds and two different hand positions.
For a Cm7 chord (root C, with notes C-Eb-G-Bb):
- **A-type (3rd on bottom)**: Eb-G-Bb-D (3-5-7-9)
- **B-type (7th on bottom)**: Bb-D-Eb-G (7-9-3-5)
The A-type has the 3rd in the lowest voice, giving a warmer, more grounded sound. The B-type has the 7th in the lowest voice, giving a more open, floating sound. Alternating between A-type and B-type as you move through a progression creates smooth voice leading with minimal hand movement.
### Practicing Rootless Voicings Through ii-V-I Progressions
[The ii](/blog/gospel-piano-chords-and-voicings-guide)-V-I progression is the perfect vehicle for practicing rootless voicings. In the key of C, the progression is Dm7 – G7 – Cmaj7. Here's one common way to voice it using rootless voicings:
- Dm7 (A-type): F-A-C-E (3-5-7-9)
- G7 (B-type): F-A-B-E (7-9-3-13)
- Cmaj7 (A-type): E-G-B-D (3-5-7-9)
Notice how the voice leading is incredibly smooth — most notes move by step or stay the same. This is the hallmark of good rootless voicing technique.
<Note>
Practice rootless voicings in all 12 keys. Start with the ii-V-I in each key, then expand to full tunes. The goal is to be able to see a chord symbol and instantly know the rootless voicing, without having to think about it.
</Note>
## Drop-2 and Drop-3 Voicings
### How Drop Voicings Are Constructed
Drop voicings are created by taking a close-position chord (where all notes are stacked in thirds within an octave) and "dropping" one note down an octave. This creates a more open, spread-out sound with wider intervals between the voices.
For a drop-2 voicing, take a four-note close-position chord (e.g., C-E-G-B for Cmaj7, stacked from bottom to top) and drop the second-highest note (G) down an octave. The result is G-C-E-B — a drop-2 voicing with a wider, more open sound.
For a drop-3 voicing, drop the third-highest note (E) down an octave: E-G-C-B. This creates an even wider spread, with the 3rd in the bass.
### Drop-2 for Close, Compact Harmony
Drop-2 voicings are the workhorse of jazz piano comping. They're compact enough to play with one hand (all four notes fit within an octave and a half), but open enough to sound full and rich. They're particularly effective for medium-tempo comping behind a soloist, where you want a supportive but unobtrusive sound.
Practice drop-2 voicings for all four inversions of each chord type (maj7, dom7, m7, m7b5, dim7). This gives you four different voicings for each chord, providing options for different musical situations and smooth voice leading.
### Drop-3 for Wider, More Open Sound
Drop-3 voicings have a wider spread than drop-2, with the lowest note separated from the upper three by a larger interval. This creates a more open, orchestral sound that works well for solo piano or when you want a bigger, more dramatic sound. The wider spread also means they're typically played with two hands, making them less [practical](/blog/piano-reharmonization-techniques-guide) for fast comping.
## Quartal and So What Voicings
### Stacking Fourths for Modern Jazz Sound
Quartal voicings are built by stacking fourths instead of thirds. Instead of C-E-G (a triad built in thirds), a quartal voicing might be C-F-B (built in fourths). These voicings have an open, modern sound that became associated with modal jazz in the 1960s.
Quartal voicings are particularly effective over modal tunes and suspended chords, where the lack of a clear major or minor third creates ambiguity and openness. They're also easy to play and move — since all the intervals are the same (fourths), you can slide the same shape up and down the keyboard.
### The So What Voicing from Miles Davis
The "So What" voicing is a specific quartal structure named after the Miles Davis tune from the album "Kind of Blue." It consists of three stacked fourths with an added top note: D-G-C-F-A for a Dm7 chord. This voicing was used extensively by Bill Evans and became a signature sound of modal jazz.
The So What voicing is particularly useful because it's ambiguous — it works over Dm7, but also over Fmaj7, Bm7b5, and other related chords. This makes it a versatile tool for modal comping and for situations where you want to create harmonic ambiguity.
### Applying Quartal Voicings to Modal Jazz
Quartal voicings shine in modal jazz contexts — tunes like "So What," "Maiden Voyage," " "Little B's Poem," and other modal pieces. In these settings, the harmony is static (one chord for many measures), and quartal voicings provide a way to create interest and movement without changing the underlying chord. By sliding quartal voicings up and down by steps, you create melodic and harmonic motion while staying within the modal framework.
<Callout>
**Quartal voicings are also great for contemporary worship and pop piano.** The open, modern sound works beautifully in genres beyond traditional jazz. Don't limit them to jazz contexts — experiment with them wherever you want a fresh, contemporary sound.
</Callout>
## Applying Voicings to Standards
### Voicing Autumn Leaves with Rootless Chords
"Autumn Leaves" is an ideal tune for practicing rootless voicings because its ii-V-I progressions in both major and minor keys provide excellent voice leading opportunities. Start by playing the melody in the right hand with rootless voicings in [the left](/blog/bossa-nova-piano-patterns-tutorial) hand. Then practice comping the entire form with rootless voicings, alternating between A-type and B-type for smooth voice leading.
### Comping with Drop-2 Voicings
For comping behind a soloist, drop-2 voicings are ideal because they're compact, rich, and easy to move between chords. Practice comping through a blues or a standard like "Blue Bossa" using only drop-2 voicings. Focus on voice leading — each chord should flow smoothly into the next with minimal hand movement.
### Creating Your Own Voicing Vocabulary
The ultimate goal is not to memorize a set of voicings but to develop your own vocabulary. As you learn and practice the voicings in this guide, experiment with modifications: add notes, remove notes, change the order, combine voicing types. Over time, you'll develop a personal sound that reflects your musical taste and influences.
Jazz piano voicings are a lifelong study. The voicings in this guide are the essential vocabulary, but they're just the beginning. As you internalize these structures and apply them to standards, you'll discover new voicings, new combinations, and new sounds. The journey from playing chords to voicing harmony is the journey from sounding like a student to sounding like a jazz pianist.
