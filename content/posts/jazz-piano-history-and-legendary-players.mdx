The piano has been at the heart of jazz since the very beginning. From the ragtime parlors of the 1890s to the modal jazz experiments of the 1960s and beyond, the instrument has evolved alongside the music, producing some of the most influential voices in jazz history.
## Ragtime and the Roots of Jazz Piano
Jazz piano begins with Scott Joplin and ragtime. In the late 1800s, Joplin's syncopated compositions — "Maple Leaf Rag," "The Entertainer" — created a sensation. Ragtime was written music, but its syncopated rhythms pointed toward the improvisational freedom that would define jazz.
Stride piano emerged as the bridge between ragtime and jazz. James P. Johnson developed the stride style, where [the left](/blog/ragtime-piano-history-and-technique) hand alternates between bass notes and chords while the right hand plays syncopated melodies. It was virtuosic, rhythmic, and deeply swinging. Johnson's student, Fats Waller, brought stride piano to popular audiences with his showmanship and songwriting.
## The Swing Era
Duke Ellington was more than a pianist — he was one of the greatest composers in American music. But his piano playing was the foundation of his orchestra's sound. His spare, bluesy comping left space for his soloists, and his compositions were tailored to the unique voices of his band members.
Art Tatum was the technical marvel of the swing era. His recordings from the 1930s and 40s still astound pianists today — his speed, harmonic sophistication, and ability to reharmonize standards on the fly were decades ahead of his time. Oscar Peterson called Tatum his greatest influence, and Tatum's legacy echoes in every jazz pianist who values virtuosity.
Count Basie proved that less could be more. His minimalist piano style — a few well-placed notes, a perfectly timed chord — defined the concept of "swing." Basie's piano was the heartbeat of his orchestra, and his approach to comping and accompaniment remains the model for jazz rhythm section playing.
## Bebop Revolution
Thelonious Monk was jazz's angular genius. His compositions — "Round Midnight," "Straight No Chaser," "Ruby My Dear" — are idiosyncratic, deeply personal, and universally revered. His piano playing was equally unique: percussive, dissonant, and full of surprising silences. Monk proved that jazz piano didn't have to be smooth to be profound.
Bud Powell was the architect of bebop piano. He translated Charlie Parker's saxophone lines to the [keyboard](/blog/baroque-keyboard-music-history-guide), playing rapid, intricate single-note lines with his right hand while comping with his left. This approach — single-note improvisation over chordal accompaniment — became the standard for modern jazz piano.
Oscar Peterson combined Tatum's virtuosity with Powell's bebop language, adding a blues sensibility and an unparalleled sense of swing. His recordings, both as a soloist and with his trio, are masterclasses in jazz piano technique, harmonic sophistication, and pure musical joy.
## Modal and Modern Jazz
Bill Evans changed jazz piano harmony forever. His work on Miles Davis's "Kind of Blue" introduced modal jazz — improvisation based on scales rather than chord progressions — to a wide audience. His own recordings, particularly "Waltz for Debby" and "Sunday at the Village Vanguard," are landmarks of introspective, interactive jazz piano.
Herbie Evans' successor in innovation was Herbie Hancock. From his work with Miles Davis in the 1960s to his funk experiments with the Headhunters in the 1970s to his ongoing explorations of electronic music, Hancock has continually reinvented what jazz piano can be.
McCoy Tyner brought modal jazz to its fullest expression with John Coltrane. His powerful left-hand chordal vamps and right-hand pentatonic runs created a sound so distinctive that it became a style in itself. Tyner's influence is heard in virtually every jazz pianist who has followed.
## [Contemporary](/blog/women-pianists-who-shaped-music-history) Jazz Piano
Today's jazz pianists draw from all of these traditions and more. Brad Mehldau brings [classical](/blog/best-piano-recordings-of-all-time) sensibility to jazz standards. Robert Glasper fuses jazz with hip-hop and R&B. Hiromi Uehara combines classical technique with rock energy and jazz freedom. The tradition is alive, evolving, and as vibrant as ever.
The story of jazz piano is ultimately a story of freedom — the freedom to improvise, to reinterpret, to make old songs new and new songs timeless. From Joplin's rags to Glasper's beats, the piano has been the perfect instrument for jazz's restless, creative spirit.
