The piano repertoire is vast, and not all of it requires large hands. Yet many pianists with smaller hands — generally meaning an comfortable span of an octave or less — feel limited by the assumption that they must conquer the big Romantic works to be "real" pianists. This is simply not true. A huge and beautiful body of music lies well within reach of smaller hands, and with thoughtful choices and a few techniques, pianists of any hand size can build a rich, satisfying repertoire.
This guide welcomes you to that repertoire, with specific recommendations and practical advice for playing beautifully regardless of your hand span.
## What "Small Hands" Means at the Piano
Hand size at the piano is usually measured by the span — the distance between the thumb and the fifth finger when the hand is fully stretched. An average adult span is around an octave (about 8 inches or 20 cm). Hands that comfortably span less than an octave, or that find large chords and wide arpeggios straining, are often described as "small."
This is not a disability or a disqualification. Many great pianists, including Alicia de Larrocha, Josef Hofmann, and more recently performers like Lang Lang (who has spoken about hand [limitations](/blog/best-piano-method-books-compared)), have built extraordinary careers with hands that were not large. The key is choosing repertoire that suits your hands and developing technique that works with, not against, your anatomy.
For a deeper dive into technique, see our guide on [technique tips for small hands](/blog/piano-technique-tips-for-small-hands) and [how to play piano with small hands](/blog/how-to-play-piano-with-small-hands).
## Baroque and Classical Repertoire
Much of the Baroque and Classical repertoire lies beautifully within smaller hands, because the instruments of those eras had narrower keys and lighter actions, and composers wrote accordingly.
### Johann Sebastian Bach
Bach's keyboard music is largely free of the large chords and stretches that challenge small hands. The *Two-Part Inventions* and *Three-Part Sinfonias* are superb studies in independence and voice-leading, mostly written in single lines and modest chords. The *Well-Tempered Clavier* offers a lifetime of repertoire, much of it accessible. The English and French Suites contain wonderful dance movements that rarely demand large spans.
### Domenico Scarlatti
Scarlatti's sonatas are a treasure for smaller hands. They are mostly in two voices, full of rhythmic vitality and brilliant figuration, and while some contain quick leaps and hand crossings, few require large chords. They are also enormous fun to play.
### Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart
Mozart's piano music, designed for the lighter, narrower-keyed instruments of his time, is generally kind to smaller hands. The sonatas (particularly the earlier ones) are full of elegant, transparent writing. The concertos, while demanding, mostly avoid the thick, chordal textures of later Romantic music.
### Joseph Haydn
Haydn's sonatas are often overlooked but are delightful, witty, and largely accessible to smaller hands. They offer wonderful opportunities for characterful, articulate playing.
## Romantic and Impressionist Selections
The [Romantic era](/blog/classical-piano-repertoire-by-era-guide) is where hand size becomes more of a factor, due to the larger chords, octaves, and stretches favored by composers like Liszt, Brahms, and Rachmaninoff. But much beautiful Romantic and Impressionist music remains accessible.
### Frédéric Chopin
While some Chopin (the big Polonaises, the *Polonaise-Fantasie*) is demanding for small hands, much is not. The *Preludes, Op. 28* are a wonderful set, with many [pieces](/blog/classical-piano-sheet-music-for-beginners) (Nos. 4, 6, 7, 9, 20) that are lyrical and manageable. The *Nocturnes* often have beautiful melodies with accompaniments that can be adapted. The *Mazurkas* are generally kind to smaller hands. See our [nocturne piano music guide](/blog/nocturne-piano-music-guide) for more.
### Robert Schumann
Schumann's *Scenes from Childhood, Op. 15* contains several short, lyrical pieces well within reach of smaller hands, including the beloved "Dreaming" (*Träumerei*). The *Album for the Young, Op. 68* is a treasure trove of accessible character pieces.
### Claude Debussy and Maurice Ravel
Much Impressionist music, with its emphasis on color and texture rather than massive chords, suits smaller hands well. Debussy's *Children's Corner Suite*, the *Petite Suite*, and many of the *Préludes* (especially Book I) are accessible. Ravel's *Sonatine* and *Pavane pour une infante défunte* are beautiful and largely manageable. See our [Impressionist era guide](/blog/piano-in-impressionist-era-guide) for context.
## 20th Century and Contemporary Works
The 20th century and beyond offer a wealth of repertoire that deliberately avoids the demands of large hands, often emphasizing rhythm, color, and texture instead.
### Béla [Bartók](/blog/piano-duet-repertoire-for-four-hands)
Bartók's *Mikrokosmos* is a progressive collection of 153 pieces, many of which are accessible to smaller hands and offer a wonderful introduction to 20th-century idioms. The earlier volumes are suitable for beginners; the later ones reach considerable difficulty but mostly through rhythm and independence rather than large spans.
### Minimalist and Contemporary Composers
The music of Philip Glass, Arvo Pärt, Ludovico Einaudi, and Yann Tiersen — much of it featured in our [new age style guide](/blog/piano-new-age-style-guide) — is built on repeating patterns and accessible textures that are well suited to smaller hands. These works offer beautiful, rewarding repertoire without large stretches.
## Techniques for Navigating Challenges
Even with well-chosen repertoire, you will encounter chords, octaves, and stretches that test your hand. Several techniques help:
### Rolling Chords
When a chord is too wide to play cleanly as a block, roll it — play the notes in quick succession from bottom to top rather than simultaneously. This is a legitimate, musical technique used by pianists of all hand sizes, especially in Romantic and Impressionist music.
### Distributing Notes Between Hands
In music where one hand has a large chord, see if notes can be taken by the other hand. This requires careful study of the score and sometimes creative redistribution, but it is a [standard](/blog/piano-jazz-standard-repertoire-list) practice.
### Using the Sustain Pedal
The pedal can sustain notes your hand must release to move elsewhere. Used thoughtfully, it allows you to voice chords and arpeggios that would otherwise be impossible to hold. See our guide on [pedaling techniques for beginners](/blog/piano-pedaling-techniques-for-beginners).
### Adapting and Simplifying
It is entirely acceptable to adapt difficult passages — omitting a note from a chord, simplifying an arpeggio — to make them playable. The goal is a musical result, not literal adherence to the score. Good teachers help students make these adaptations intelligently.
### [Building](/blog/czerny-piano-exercises-for-technique) Flexibility
Over time, careful stretching exercises (done gently, never to the point of pain) can increase your comfortable span. Our guide on [technique tips for small hands](/blog/piano-technique-tips-for-small-hands) covers safe approaches.
## Building Your Repertoire
The key is to build a repertoire that plays to your strengths. For more structured guidance, see our [repertoire guide by skill level](/blog/piano-repertoire-guide-by-skill-level). Remember that the beauty of a performance lies in the music, not in the size of the chords. Some of the most beloved piano recordings ever made were performed by pianists with modest hand spans, playing music that suited them beautifully.
Your hands are not a limitation — they are a particular instrument with particular strengths. Choose music that lets those strengths shine, develop technique that works with your anatomy, and you will find that the piano's vast repertoire has more than enough to offer a lifetime of joyful music-making.
