There's a temptation, when learning a new piece, to put both hands together right away. It feels like progress — you're playing the piece, after all. But this is one of the most common and costly mistakes pianists make. Practicing hands separately isn't a beginner crutch; it's a technique used by concert pianists on the most advanced [repertoire](/blog/how-to-build-piano-finger-independence). It's simply the fastest, most reliable way to learn anything complex.
Here's why hands-separate practice works so well, and how to do it so it actually speeds your progress rather than slowing it.
## Why Hands Separately Works
When you play hands together from the start, your brain has to juggle two independent streams of information — two rhythms, two sets of notes, two fingerings — simultaneously. The result is that neither hand gets the attention it needs, and you build in mistakes that take far longer to unlearn.
Practicing one hand at a time lets you give that hand your full attention:
- **Learn each hand thoroughly** before combining them
- **Catch mistakes early** when they're easy to fix
- **Build muscle memory** that's solid before [adding](/blog/how-to-play-piano-by-ear) complexity
- **Reduce cognitive load** so you can focus on nuance, not just survival
<Callout type="note">
Think of it like learning to juggle. You don't start with three balls — you master one, then two, then three. Hands-separate practice is the one-ball stage.
</Callout>
## The Right Way to Practice Hands Separately
### 1. Start with the Hand That's Harder
Most pieces have a harder hand (often the left). Begin there so the easier hand isn't waiting while you struggle. Once the hard hand is comfortable, the easy hand comes quickly.
### 2. Practice to Mastery, Not Just Familiarity
Don't just "try" a hand a few times. Practice it until it's fluent — you can play it accurately, with good fingering, at tempo (or close to it), and with musical shaping. Half-learning a hand means hands-together will still be a struggle.
### 3. Use a Metronome
Hands-separate practice is the perfect time to build steady rhythm. Use a metronome from the start with each hand, so when you combine them, both hands share a reliable pulse.
### 4. Practice in Small Sections
Don't run through the whole piece one-handed. Work in small chunks — a phrase or two — until each is solid, then connect them. This is faster, not slower.
## How Much Is Enough?
A useful guideline: each hand should be able to play its part accurately and comfortably at about 80% of the final tempo before you put hands together. If you combine hands when neither is secure, you're practicing struggle, not music.
<Callout type="tip">
If hands-together falls apart, that's a signal to go back to hands-separate. It's not a failure — it's the method telling you a hand needs more work.
</Callout>
## When to Use Hands Together
Hands-separate isn't the only tool. Once each hand is solid, you should combine them — but combine them slowly and in small sections, not by running the whole piece. The progression is:
1. Right hand alone — to mastery
2. Left hand alone — to mastery
3. Hands together, very slowly, small sections
4. Gradually increase tempo and section size
The mistake is jumping from step 2 to a full-tempo run-through. Resist it.
## Special Cases
### Chordal or Hymn-Like Music
When both hands move together in block chords, hands-separate is less critical, but still useful for learning each voice's notes cleanly.
### Contrapuntal Music (Bach)
Hands-separate is essential. Each voice is independent and deserves to be understood on its own. Many teachers also recommend practicing each voice alone, then combining two, then all.
### Accompaniment Patterns
If one hand has a repetitive pattern (like an arpeggiated left hand), master that pattern alone until it's automatic. Then the other hand can ride on top without competing for attention.
## Signs You're Doing It Right
- Each hand plays its part with attention to fingering, rhythm, and dynamics
- You can play a hand's part from memory before combining
- Hands-together feels like a meeting of two prepared partners, not a collision
- Mistakes are rare and easy to pinpoint
## Common Mistakes
- **Rushing to hands-together** — the most common and costly error
- **Practicing hands-separate without a metronome** — rhythm then falters when combined
- **Not practicing the "easy" hand enough** — even simple parts need to be automatic
- **Skipping hands-separate on "easy" pieces** — the habit pays off most when built consistently
## A Sample Workflow
For a new piece:
1. Sight-read each hand separately once to get the lay of the land
2. Choose the harder hand; practice a small section to mastery with metronome
3. Practice the same section with the other hand to mastery
4. Combine hands, very slowly, on that section
5. Repeat for the next section, then connect
This workflow feels slow in the moment but is dramatically faster overall, because you avoid the endless re-learning that comes from sloppy hands-together attempts.
Hands-separate practice is the single most underused technique among amateur pianists and the most relied-upon among professionals. It's not a sign of weakness — it's a sign of intelligence. Master each hand, combine them deliberately, and you'll learn [pieces faster](/blog/how-to-memorize-piano-pieces-faster), more securely, and more musically than you ever have before.
