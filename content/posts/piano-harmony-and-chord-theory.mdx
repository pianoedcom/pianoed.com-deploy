Once you know your basic major and minor chords, a whole world opens up: the world of harmony. Harmony is how chords relate to one another, how they progress, and how they create emotional movement in music. For intermediate pianists, understanding harmony transforms playing from memorizing songs to understanding why they work — and how to create your own.
This guide takes you beyond the basics into diatonic harmony, chord progressions, inversions, and [extensions](/blog/piano-chord-substitutions-guide), with warmth and clarity.
## Diatonic Chords: The Family of a Key
In any major key, seven naturally occurring chords are built from the scale. These are called **diatonic chords**, and they are numbered with Roman numerals based on their scale degree. In C major, the diatonic chords are:
| Roman Numeral | Chord | Type |
|---------------|-------|------|
| I | C major | Major |
| ii | D minor | Minor |
| iii | E minor | Minor |
| IV | F major | Major |
| V | G major (or G7) | Major / Dominant |
| vi | A minor | Minor |
| vii° | B diminished | Diminished |
These seven chords form the harmonic vocabulary of the key. Most songs in a major key use only these chords, and understanding their relationships is the key to understanding harmony.
<Callout title="Why Roman numerals?">
Roman numerals let us talk about chord relationships in any key. "I–IV–V" means the same thing in C major (C-F-G) as in G major (G-C-D). Learning to think in numerals frees you from any single key.
</Callout>
## Common Chord Progressions
Certain progressions appear again and again because they simply sound right. Here are the most important:
- **I–V–vi–IV**: The "pop [progression](/blog/piano-improvisation-techniques-guide)" — heard in countless songs across genres.
- **I–IV–V**: The backbone of blues, rock, and folk.
- **ii–V–I**: The quintessential jazz progression; learn it in every key.
- **vi–IV–I–V**: A emotional, anthemic progression common in pop ballads.
Learning these progressions in multiple keys builds a harmonic intuition that applies to any style. For more, see our guide on [piano chord progressions every player should know](/blog/piano-chord-progressions-every-player-should-know).
## Inversions: Smoother [Voice Leading](/blog/piano-voicing-techniques-for-chords)
A chord inversion is simply a different ordering of the same notes. A C major triad (C-E-G) can be played as:
- **Root position**: C-E-G (C on the bottom)
- **First inversion**: E-G-C (E on the bottom)
- **Second inversion**: G-C-E (G on the bottom)
Inversions matter because they allow smoother connections between chords. Instead of jumping between root positions, you can move the inner voices by step, creating a more musical, flowing sound. This is called **voice leading**, and it is the secret to professional-sounding accompaniment.
<Tip>Practice playing the I–IV–V progression using inversions so that your hand moves as little as possible. The smoother the voice leading, the more polished the sound.</Tip>
## Seventh Chords and Extensions
Adding a fourth note to a triad creates a **seventh chord**, which adds color and tension. The most important is the **dominant seventh** (V7), which strongly pulls toward the tonic. In C major, G7 (G-B-D-F) resolves to C major with satisfying finality.
Beyond sevenths, jazz and contemporary music use **extensions**:
- **9ths, 11ths, 13ths**: Notes stacked above the seventh, adding richness and complexity.
- **Altered chords**: Sharped or flatted extensions that create colorful tension.
For a deeper dive into jazz voicings, see our guide on [jazz piano voicings for intermediate players](/blog/jazz-piano-voicings-for-intermediate-players).
## Minor Key Harmony
Minor keys have a richer harmonic palette because of their variable sixth and seventh scale degrees. The natural, harmonic, and melodic minor [scales](/blog/piano-theory-basics-for-beginners) each create different chord possibilities. The harmonic minor, with its raised seventh, produces the powerful V7 chord that gives minor-key music its dramatic pull.
Common minor progressions include i–iv–V7–i and i–VI–III–VII. Minor harmony tends to sound more emotional and darker, making it a favorite for jazz, film music, and ballads.
## How to Practice Harmony
Here is a structured approach to internalizing harmony:
1. **Learn the diatonic chords** in C, G, F, and D major. Play them in root position first.
2. **Practice common progressions** (I–IV–V, I–vi–IV–V) in multiple keys.
3. **Add inversions** to smooth voice leading.
4. **Introduce seventh chords**, starting with V7.
5. **Experiment with extensions** [in jazz](/blog/piano-modes-and-scales-explained) and pop styles.
For inversions specifically, see our guide on [piano chord inversions explained](/blog/piano-chord-inversions-explained).
## Harmony as Musical Storytelling
Harmony is how music tells a story. A progression creates tension and release, expectation and surprise — the emotional architecture beneath every melody. When you understand harmony, you stop merely playing notes and start shaping meaning. Whether you are accompanying a song, improvising, or composing, harmony is your palette, and the piano is your canvas. The more you learn, the more colors you have to paint with.
