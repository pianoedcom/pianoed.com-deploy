Notes on a page tell you which pitches to play and how long to hold them. But music isn't just pitches and durations — it's also about how each note is touched. Is it smooth or detached? Emphasized or gentle? Held for its full value or slightly cut? These instructions come from articulation marks, the small symbols above and below the notes that shape the character of the music.
Learning articulation marks turns sheet music from a list of notes into a detailed performance guide. Here's what each mark means and how to play it.
## Why Articulation Marks Matter
Articulation is what separates a mechanical performance from a musical one. The same sequence of notes can sound lyrical, playful, or aggressive depending on articulation. Composers use these marks to communicate their intentions precisely, and respecting them is part of playing a piece faithfully.
- **Slurs** tell you to play smoothly
- **Staccato dots** tell you to play short
- **Accents** tell you to emphasize
- **Tenuto lines** tell you to hold fully
<Callout type="note">
Think of articulation marks as the composer's handwriting instructions — they tell you not just what to play, but how to play it.
</Callout>
## The Slur
A slur is a curved line connecting two or more notes of [different](/blog/piano-melody-ornamentation-techniques) pitches. It means play legato — smooth and connected, with no gap between notes.
- **On the piano**: overlap the notes slightly, transferring weight from one finger to the next
- **Phrasing slurs**: longer slurs over many notes indicate a musical phrase — play smoothly and shape the line with a slight rise and fall in dynamics
- **Slurs with dots under them**: portato — halfway between legato and staccato, notes are connected but slightly separated
## Staccato
A staccato mark is a small dot above or below a note. It means play the note short and detached.
- **On the piano**: play the key quickly and release immediately, creating a clear silence after the note
- **Keep it light**: staccato should sound crisp, not harsh — use a slight wrist bounce
- **Staccatissimo** (a small wedge): even shorter and more detached than standard staccato
## Accent
An accent mark (>) above a note means play that note louder or with more emphasis than the surrounding notes.
- **On the piano**: give the note a firmer attack, but don't hammer it
- **Context matters**: an accent is relative — it's louder than its neighbors, not necessarily loud in absolute terms
- **Marcato** (^): a stronger, more emphatic accent
## Tenuto
A tenuto mark is a small horizontal line above or below a note. It means hold the note for its full value, with a slight emphasis.
- **On the piano**: play the note smoothly and fully, neither short nor accented
- **Tenuto often implies legato** within a phrase, with a gentle stress on the marked note
- It's the quietest articulation — a "hold and sustain" instruction
## Combinations
Articulation marks often combine:
- **Staccato + accent**: short and loud — a sharp, emphatic note
- **Tenuto + staccato**: a paradoxical "portato" — notes that are slightly separated but held near full value
- **Slur + staccato dots**: portato — connected but slightly detached
These combinations give composers fine control over character.
## Fermata
A fermata is a small curved line with a dot under it, placed over a note or rest. It means hold the note longer than its written value, at the performer's discretion.
- **On the piano**: sustain the note (often with the pedal) for a moment that feels right
- **Fermatas usually appear at phrase endings** or important structural points
- The length is interpretive — there's no fixed rule, but it should feel like a meaningful pause
## [Reading](/blog/piano-duet-repertoire-for-four-hands) Articulation in Context
When you learn a piece, scan for articulation marks early:
1. **Note the slurs** — they define phrases; shape each phrase as a single musical line
2. **Watch staccato and tenuto** — they contrast with the slurs and add character
3. **Find the accents** — they highlight important notes and rhythmic stresses
4. **Observe fermatas** — they mark moments to breathe and linger
<Callout type="tip">
Don't treat articulation marks as optional. They're the difference between playing the notes and playing the music. A piece played with correct articulation sounds intentional; the same notes played uniformly sound flat.
</Callout>
## Practice [Approach](/blog/classical-piano-sheet-music-for-beginners)
- **Isolate a phrase** and play it with the marked articulation, slowly
- **Exaggerate at first** — make staccatos very short, slurs very smooth, to feel the contrast
- **Then refine** — bring the articulation to a natural, musical level
- **Listen** — the articulation should be audible; if you can't hear the difference, it's not there
## [Common Mistakes](/blog/piano-ornamentation-and-trills-guide)
- **Ignoring marks entirely** — the most common error; uniform playing flattens character
- **Over-doing staccato** — too short and harsh; keep it light
- **Under-doing slurs** — gaps between notes that should be connected
- **Missing accents** — accented notes that sound like all the others
- **Rushing fermatas** — they're meant to be held, not hurried through
Articulation marks are the composer's voice telling you how the music should feel. Learn each mark, respect it in your playing, and your performances will carry the character the composer intended. It's a small layer of detail that transforms notes into music.
