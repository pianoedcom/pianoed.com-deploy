The piano concerto is one of the grandest forms in all of [classical](/blog/best-piano-recordings-of-all-time) music. The combination of a solo pianist with a full orchestra creates a musical dialogue that is by turns intimate and thunderous — a conversation between an individual voice and a collective force. From the elegant classical concertos of Mozart to the towering romantic masterpieces of Rachmaninoff, the piano concerto has been a vehicle for some of the greatest music ever written.
This guide traces [the history of the piano](/blog/history-of-the-piano-evolution) concerto from its origins to the present day, exploring how the form evolved and highlighting the essential concertos every piano lover should know.
## Origins of the Piano Concerto
### From Concerto Grosso to Solo Concerto
The concerto as a musical form originated in the Baroque era with the concerto grosso, in which a small group of soloists (the concertino) contrasted with a larger ensemble (the ripieno). Composers like Corelli, Handel, and Bach wrote concerti grossi that established the fundamental principle of the concerto: the dialogue between individual and ensemble.
In the early 18th century, the solo concerto emerged as a distinct form. Instead of a group of soloists, a single instrument was pitted against the orchestra. The violin was the first instrument to be featured in solo concertos, with composers like Vivaldi writing hundreds of them. The keyboard concerto followed, with Bach's harpsichord concertos representing some of the earliest examples.
### Mozart's Pioneering Piano Concertos
Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart was the composer who truly established the piano concerto as a major musical form. Mozart wrote 27 piano concertos, and they represent some of his finest and most personal music. Unlike many composers who wrote primarily for patrons, Mozart wrote his piano concertos for his own performance — they were the centerpiece of his subscription concerts in Vienna.
Mozart's piano concertos established the classical concerto form: three movements (fast-slow-fast), with the first movement using a modified sonata form that includes an orchestral exposition (presenting the themes before the soloist enters), a solo exposition (where the pianist presents and expands the themes), a development, and a recapitulation. This structure would remain the template for piano concertos for over a century.
Mozart's concertos are remarkable for their balance between soloist and orchestra. The piano is not merely accompanied by the orchestra — the two are equal partners in a musical conversation. The slow movements, in particular, contain some of Mozart's most deeply expressive music, with the piano singing melodies of heartbreaking beauty.
<Callout>
**Mozart's Piano Concerto No. 20 in D minor, K. 466, and No. 23 in A major, K. 488, are essential listening.** These concertos represent the pinnacle of Mozart's concerto writing and demonstrate the emotional range and structural sophistication of the form.
</Callout>
### The Rise of the Virtuoso Performer-Composer
After Mozart, the piano concerto became increasingly associated with virtuosity. The 19th century saw the rise of the performer-composer — pianists who wrote concertos to showcase their own extraordinary abilities. This trend began with Beethoven and continued through Chopin, Liszt, and Rachmaninoff, producing some of the most spectacular and beloved concertos in the repertoire.
## Beethoven and the Romantic Concerto
### Beethoven's Transformation of the Form
Ludwig van Beethoven wrote five piano concertos, and they trace a remarkable evolution from the classical model to something more personal and dramatic. Beethoven's first two concertos are firmly in the Mozartian [tradition](/blog/baroque-keyboard-music-history-guide), but by the third concerto (in C minor), Beethoven was expanding the form's emotional scope and structural ambition.
The Fourth Piano Concerto (in G major, Op. 58) is a landmark in the form's history. It begins not with the orchestra, as was traditional, but with the solo piano — a quiet, lyrical opening that was revolutionary for its time. The Fifth Piano Concerto (in E-flat major, Op. 73, "Emperor") is the grandest and most powerful of Beethoven's concertos, with a heroic scale that would influence romantic composers for decades.
Beethoven also transformed the role of the soloist. In Mozart's concertos, the soloist and orchestra are partners. In Beethoven's, the soloist is a hero — a protagonist struggling against and ultimately triumphing over the orchestral forces. This dramatic conception of the concerto would define the romantic concerto.
### Chopin, Schumann, and Liszt Concertos
The romantic generation produced piano concertos of great emotional depth and virtuosity. [Frédéric Chopin](/blog/romantic-era-piano-composers-guide) wrote two piano concertos (No. 1 in E minor, No. 2 in F minor) that are beloved for their poetic slow movements and brilliant piano writing, though they have been criticized for their relatively modest orchestral parts.
Robert Schumann's Piano Concerto in A minor, Op. 54, is a masterpiece of romantic concerto writing, with a deeply integrated relationship between soloist and orchestra and a lyrical quality that is quintessentially Schumann.
Franz Liszt's two piano concertos are spectacular showcases for virtuosity. The First Concerto in E-flat major is a single-movement work that cycles through multiple tempos and moods, while the Second Concerto in A major is more lyrical and integrated. Both demonstrate Liszt's revolutionary approach to concerto form, breaking free from the traditional three-movement structure.
### The Cadenza and Its Evolving Role
The cadenza — an unaccompanied solo passage near the end of the first movement — was an important feature of the classical concerto. In Mozart's time, the cadenza was improvised by the performer, providing a moment of spontaneous creativity within the structured form. Beethoven began writing out his own cadenzas, and by the romantic era, most composers wrote fully composed cadenzas rather than leaving them to the performer's improvisation.
<Note>
**The cadenza serves a dramatic function.** It's the moment when the soloist is most alone — stripped of orchestral support, the pianist must sustain interest and build tension using only their own resources. Great cadenzas feel like the emotional climax of the movement, even though the music continues afterward.
</Note>
## The Golden Age of Piano Concertos
### Rachmaninoff and [Tchaikovsky](/blog/great-piano-competitions-history-guide) Masterpieces
The late romantic period produced some of the most beloved piano concertos ever written. Pyotr Tchaikovsky's Piano Concerto No. 1 in B-flat minor, Op. 23, is one of the most famous and frequently performed concertos in the repertoire. Its dramatic opening chords and sweeping melodies have made it a favorite of audiences and pianists alike.
Sergei Rachmaninoff wrote four piano concertos, all of which are masterpieces. The Second Concerto in C minor, Op. 18, is perhaps the most beloved — a work of profound emotional depth that Rachmaninoff wrote after recovering from a period of depression and creative block. The Third Concerto in D minor, Op. 30, is one of the most technically demanding concertos ever written, famous for its fiendish difficulty and the legendary performances by pianists like [Vladimir Horowitz](/blog/famous-classical-pianists-who-changed-music).
### Brahms and Grieg Contributions
Johannes Brahms wrote two piano concertos that are monumental in scale and intellectual depth. The First Concerto in D minor, Op. 15, is a stormy, dramatic work that took Brahms years to complete. The Second Concerto in B-flat major, Op. 83, is one of the longest and most symphonic piano concertos, with four movements instead of the usual three.
Edvard Grieg's Piano Concerto in A minor, Op. 16, is the most famous Norwegian classical composition and one of the most beloved romantic concertos. Its lyrical melodies, influenced by Norwegian folk music, and brilliant piano writing have made it a perennial favorite.
### The Concerto as a Showcase for Virtuosity
By the late romantic period, the piano concerto had become the ultimate showcase for pianistic virtuosity. Composers wrote concertos that pushed the limits of what was possible on the piano, with massive chords, rapid octaves, enormous leaps, and complex polyrhythms. The concerto was not just a musical form — it was an athletic event, a test of the pianist's technical prowess and endurance.
## [20th Century](/blog/women-pianists-who-shaped-music-history) and Modern Concertos
### Prokofiev, Bartók, and Shostakovich
The 20th century brought new approaches to the piano concerto. Sergei Prokofiev wrote five piano concertos that are modern in their harmonic language but rooted in the romantic tradition of virtuosity. His Third Concerto in C major, Op. 26, is the most popular, with its driving rhythms and brilliant piano writing.
Béla Bartók's three piano concertos are among the most important 20th-century works in the form. They combine folk music influences, modernist harmony, and innovative orchestration. The Second and Third Concertos are particularly noted for their rhythmic complexity and integration of folk elements.
Dmitri Shostakovich wrote two piano concertos that are notable for their accessibility and wit. The First Concerto, Op. 35, includes a prominent part for solo trumpet, creating a playful dialogue between piano and trumpet. The Second Concerto, Op. 102, is a lyrical, melodic work that is one of the composer's most popular pieces.
### Gershwin's Rhapsody in Blue
George Gershwin's "Rhapsody in Blue" occupies a unique place in the concerto repertoire. Blending classical concerto form with jazz rhythms and harmonies, it represents a distinctly American approach to the piano concerto. Its opening clarinet glissando and subsequent piano passages have become iconic, and the work remains one of the most popular pieces of American classical music.
### [Contemporary](/blog/jazz-piano-history-and-legendary-players) Composers and the Concerto Today
Contemporary composers continue to write piano concertos, and the form remains vital. Composers like Arvo Pärt, Alfred Schnittke, and Thomas Adès have written concertos that push the form in new directions, exploring new harmonic languages, extended techniques, and new relationships between soloist and orchestra. The piano concerto is not a museum piece — it is a living form that continues to evolve.
<Callout>
**The piano concerto remains one of the most exciting forms in classical music.** The combination of a single pianist with a full orchestra creates a dramatic tension that is unique in music — the individual against the collective, the personal voice against the mass. This tension is as compelling today as it was in Mozart's time.
</Callout>

### How to Listen to and Appreciate Concertos
When listening to a piano concerto, pay attention to the dialogue between the soloist and the orchestra. Notice how the themes are introduced by the orchestra and then taken up by the piano, how the piano elaborates and transforms them, and how the two forces interact throughout the piece. Listen for the cadenza — the moment when the orchestra falls silent and the pianist is alone. And notice the emotional arc of the entire work — the journey from the opening notes to the final cadence.
The piano concerto is one of the great achievements of Western music. From Mozart's elegant classical works to Rachmaninoff's towering romantic masterpieces to the innovative concertos of the 20th and 21st centuries, the form has produced some of the most beloved and enduring music ever written. Exploring this repertoire is a lifelong journey — one that will reward you with some of the most thrilling and moving musical experiences available.
