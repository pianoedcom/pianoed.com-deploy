A pop song gives you a skeleton — a melody, a chord progression, a basic structure. Your job as a pianist is to turn that skeleton into a living, breathing solo arrangement. This is one of the most creative and satisfying things you can do at the piano: take a song you love and make it yours.
Unlike classical music, where every note is written out, pop arrangement is about making real-time decisions. What do you play in [the left](/blog/bossa-nova-piano-patterns-tutorial) hand? How do you voice the chords? Where do you put the melody? This guide walks through the techniques that turn a chord chart into a complete solo piano arrangement.
## The Three Layers of Solo Piano
A solo piano arrangement needs to convey three things simultaneously:
1. **Melody** — the tune, usually in the right hand
2. **[Harmony](/blog/jazz-piano-voicings-for-intermediate-players)** — the chords, shared between both hands
3. **Bass** — the root motion, usually in the left hand
The challenge is doing all three with only two hands and ten fingers. Here's how.
<Callout type="note">
Think of your left hand as the bass player and your right hand as the [singer](/blog/contemporary-piano-music-genres-overview) and guitarist combined. The left hand provides the foundation; the right hand provides the melody and the chord color above it.
</Callout>
## Technique 1: Melody + Broken Chord Accompaniment
This is the most common [approach](/blog/gospel-piano-chords-and-voicings-guide) for pop ballads. The right hand plays the melody with fingers 3-4-5 while the lower fingers (1-2) play chord tones underneath. The left hand plays the root (or root-fifth) in octaves.
**Right hand:** Melody on top, chord tones (root, 3rd, 5th) below
**Left hand:** Root in octaves, or root-fifth-root [pattern](/blog/piano-blues-scale-patterns-and-licks)
This creates a full, flowing sound that works for ballads like Adele's "Someone Like You" or Sam Smith's "Stay With Me."
## Technique 2: Melody + Block Chords
For a more rhythmic, upbeat sound, play the melody in the right hand with block chords underneath. The left hand plays the bass on beat 1 and holds.
**Right hand:** Melody on top, full chord (3-4 notes) below
**Left hand:** Root on beat 1, sustain
This works well for mid-tempo pop songs like John Legend's "All of Me."
## Technique 3: Arpeggiated Left Hand + Melody
Instead of holding chords, arpeggiate the left hand — play the chord tones one at a time as a rolling pattern while the right hand plays the melody.
**Left hand:** Root-fifth-third-fifth (or root-third-fifth-octave) pattern
**Right hand:** Melody only
This is the classic "pop ballad" sound — think "River Flows in You" by Yiruma or "A Thousand Years" by Christina Perri.
## Technique 4: Stride-Style Bass
For a more energetic, vintage sound, use a stride bass pattern in the left hand: root on beat 1, chord on beat 2, root on beat 3, chord on beat 4. The right hand plays the melody with chord tones.
**Left hand:** Root (beat 1) → chord (beat 2) → root (beat 3) → chord (beat 4)
**Right hand:** Melody + chord tones
This works for upbeat pop, rock piano, and anything with a swing feel.
## Technique 5: Two-Handed Voicings with Melody on Top
For a richer, more modern sound, spread the chord across both hands. The melody is the top note of the right hand, and the rest of the chord is distributed between the remaining right-hand fingers and the left hand.
**Right hand:** Melody (finger 5) + upper chord tones (fingers 1-3)
**Left hand:** Lower chord tones + root
This is the approach used by many contemporary singer-songwriter pianists. It creates a wide, full sound that fills the keyboard without being muddy.
<Callout type="tip">
The secret to two-handed voicings is keeping the melody clearly on top. If the melody gets buried in the chord, the arrangement sounds muddy. Play the melody finger slightly louder than the rest.
</Callout>
## Adding Interest and Variation
### Intro and Outro
Don't just start on the verse. Create a short intro — arpeggiate the first chord, or play a simplified version of the chorus melody. For the outro, repeat the final chord with a slow arpeggio, or add a countermelody.
### Dynamic Build
A great arrangement builds dynamically. Start sparse (melody + single bass notes), then add chord tones, then add rhythmic patterns, then reach a full climax in the chorus. Pull back for the bridge, then build again for the final chorus.
### Reharmonization
Change the chords to create interest. Replace a major chord with a major 7th. Add a secondary dominant before a chord. Substitute a relative minor for a major. These small changes make a familiar song sound fresh.
### Rhythmic Displacement
Instead of playing the melody exactly as written, slightly delay or anticipate notes. This "pushing and pulling" (rubato) gives the arrangement a more personal, expressive feel.
### Fills and Runs
Between vocal phrases (or when the melody holds a long note), add fills — quick runs, arpeggios, or melodic fragments that connect one [phrase](/blog/jazz-piano-improvisation-basics-guide) to the next. These are the pianist's equivalent of a guitarist's solo between vocal lines.
## A Step-by-Step Arrangement Process
1. **Learn the melody** — Play it alone with your right hand until it's comfortable
2. **Learn the chords** — Play them as simple block chords in the left hand
3. **Combine melody + chords** — Melody in right hand, block chords in left
4. **Add bass motion** — Replace block chords with root-fifth or arpeggiated patterns
5. **Add voicings** — Expand the chords to include 7ths, 9ths, and suspensions
6. **Add dynamics** — Decide where the arrangement builds and where it pulls back
7. **Add fills** — Insert melodic fragments between phrases
8. **Add intro and outro** — Create a beginning and ending
9. **Play it through** — Perform the complete arrangement, adjusting as needed
<Callout type="note">
Don't try to make the perfect arrangement on the first try. Start simple — melody and bass — and add layers gradually. Each layer you add should serve the song, not show off the pianist. If a layer doesn't improve the arrangement, remove it.
</Callout>
## Common Arrangement Mistakes
- **Too many notes** — A full chord in every hand on every beat sounds muddy. Leave space.
- **Melody buried** — If you can't hear the tune, the arrangement has failed. The melody must always be the loudest element.
- **No dynamic range** — An arrangement that's the same volume throughout is boring. Build and release.
- **Over-complicating** — The best pop arrangements are often the simplest. Don't add complexity that doesn't serve the song.
- **Ignoring the bass** — The bass is the foundation. If it's weak, everything above it sounds unstable.
