Most pianists spend their lives keeping things off the strings — dust, debris, anything that would interfere with the pure tone. The prepared piano does the opposite: it places objects deliberately on and between the strings to transform the instrument's sound entirely. Screws, bolts, rubber, paper, plastic — these mundane materials turn the piano into a percussion ensemble, a gamelan, a collection of strange and beautiful new voices. Invented in the twentieth century, the prepared piano is one of the most radical and imaginative ideas in the instrument's [history](/blog/history-of-the-piano-evolution).
## What Is a Prepared Piano?
A prepared piano is an acoustic piano whose sound has been altered by placing objects on or between the strings. The preparations change the strings' vibration, producing new timbres — metallic buzzes, mellow thuds, bell-like rings, drum-like strikes — that sound nothing like a conventional piano. The same instrument becomes many [instruments](/blog/baroque-keyboard-music-history-guide) depending on the preparations.
The preparations are specified by the composer: which strings, which objects, where exactly placed, and how tightly. A score for prepared piano includes a preparation chart describing each modification in detail, so a performer can reproduce the intended sounds.
<Callout type="note">
The prepared piano is not improvisation with random objects. It is a precisely specified instrument, with each preparation chosen for its acoustic effect. The preparation chart is as important as the notes themselves.
</Callout>
## The Origins: John Cage
The prepared piano is the creation of the American composer John Cage, who devised it in 1938–1940. Working with dancer Syvilla Fort, Cage needed a percussion accompaniment but had only a piano in a small performance space. Unable to fit a percussion ensemble, he began placing objects on the piano's strings to transform it into a one-person percussion orchestra.
Cage refined the idea over several works, most notably the Sonatas and Interludes (1946–48), a substantial cycle that explores the prepared piano's full range of colors. His preparations — using screws, bolts, nuts, rubber, and plastic placed at specific points on specific strings — produced a carefully tuned palette of percussive, gamelan-like, and exotic tones.
Cage's invention was both practical and philosophical. It expanded what a piano could be and challenged the idea of fixed instrumental identity — a theme that ran through much of his later work.
## How Preparations Work
Different objects produce different effects depending on where and how they're placed:
### Screws, Bolts, and Nuts
Placed between the strings, metal hardware buzzes and rattles, producing metallic, percussive tones. The position along the string changes the pitch and character of the resulting sound. Tight placements produce more defined pitches; loose ones create wilder rattles.
### Rubber and Plastic
Rubber wedges or strips mute the strings, producing soft, muffled thuds or bell-like tones depending on placement. Rubber near the hammers' strike point changes the attack; rubber further along the string alters the sustain and pitch.
### Paper and Cardboard
Thin paper woven between strings adds a subtle buzz, softening the tone and adding texture. Heavier cardboard mutes more dramatically.
### Erasers and Felt
Placed on the strings, these dampen vibration, producing muted, percussive sounds with little pitch.
### Baskets and Containers
Larger objects resting on the strings can produce complex, resonant tones — sometimes used to create gong or drum effects.
The art of preparation is in choosing the right object for the desired sound and placing it precisely. Small changes in position produce dramatically different results.
## The Preparation Chart
A prepared piano score includes a chart specifying each preparation. A typical chart lists:
- The note name and the specific string(s)
- The object to be placed
- The precise position along the string (often measured from the damper or a reference point)
- How tightly the object should be placed
Preparing a piano can take an hour or more, and the result must be checked note by note. A poorly placed preparation sounds wrong or can damage the string.
## Playing the Prepared Piano
Once prepared, the piano plays much like a normal piano — the same keys, the same action — but the sounds are transformed. Some preparations make certain notes nearly unplayable or drastically altered; the performer must adapt. Dynamics, pedaling, and touch all interact with the preparations in new ways.
Prepared piano music often explores the instrument's percussive and textural possibilities rather than conventional melody and [harmony](/blog/piano-cadenza-guide-and-examples). The result can be meditative, eerie, playful, or violent, depending on the preparations and the music.
## Care and Risks
Preparing a piano carries real risks, and care is essential:
- **Don't damage the strings** — sharp or heavy objects can scratch, dent, or break strings. Use smooth, appropriately sized objects
- **Don't interfere with the action** — preparations must not block the hammers or dampers
- **Don't overtighten** — forcing objects between strings can bend or break them
- **Protect the finish** — resting objects on the soundboard or frame can scratch
- **Remove preparations carefully** — after a performance, remove all objects and check that the piano returns to normal
<Callout type="warning">
Never prepare a piano without permission from its owner. Preparations can damage strings and finish, and many pianos — especially valuable or historic instruments — should not be prepared at all. Use a designated or less precious instrument for prepared piano work.
</Callout>
Many [performers](/blog/piano-concerto-history-guide) use a specific piano for prepared work and keep a separate instrument for conventional playing. Some venues own a piano set aside for prepared performances.
## The Prepared Piano's Influence
Cage's prepared piano opened a door. It showed that the piano was not a fixed instrument but a sound source that could be reshaped. This idea influenced generations of composers and led to a broader exploration of [extended piano techniques](/blog/extended-piano-techniques-guide) — plucking strings, bowing them, striking the frame, using the piano as a resonant object.
The prepared piano also fits within the wider story of [twentieth-century piano music](/blog/piano-in-20th-century-music-guide), where composers continually sought new sounds from the instrument — from inside-the-piano techniques to electronic modification. The prepared piano stands as one of the most imaginative and influential of these explorations.
## Prepared Piano in Contemporary Music
Though most associated with Cage, the prepared piano has been taken up by many composers and performers:
- **Concert music** — composers continue to write for prepared piano, exploring new preparations and combinations
- **Film and experimental music** — the prepared piano's eerie, otherworldly tones suit atmospheric and experimental contexts
- **Cross-genre exploration** — some jazz and improvising pianists use selective preparations for textural color
The prepared piano remains a living technique, not just a historical curiosity.
## Trying Prepared Piano
For curious pianists, trying prepared piano is rewarding but should be done carefully:
1. **Use a piano you can afford to experiment with** — not a treasured or valuable instrument
2. **Start with gentle preparations** — rubber and paper are safer than metal hardware
3. **Follow a published preparation chart** — Cage's Sonatas and Interludes chart is a classic starting point
4. **Place objects gently** — never force anything between strings
5. **Remove everything afterward** and confirm the piano plays normally
6. **Document your preparations** so you can reproduce the sounds
The [experience](/blog/great-piano-competitions-history-guide) of hearing a familiar piano transformed is unforgettable. It changes how you think about the instrument — its strings, its body, its possibilities.
## The Prepared Piano's Place in Piano History
The prepared piano is a reminder that the piano, for all its tradition, has never been a fixed, finished instrument. From its invention by Cristofori to the [evolution of its design](/blog/evolution-of-piano-design-through-centuries) to the digital and hybrid pianos of today, the piano has always been reshaped by the people who play it. The prepared piano is one of the boldest acts of reshaping — turning the most refined of instruments into a percussion orchestra with a handful of hardware and a lot of imagination.
It is, in its way, a love letter to the piano's possibilities — proof that even an instrument centuries old still has new voices waiting to be heard.
