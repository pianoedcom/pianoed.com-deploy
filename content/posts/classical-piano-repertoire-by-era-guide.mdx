The piano repertoire spans roughly three centuries, from the harpsichord works of the Baroque era to the minimalist compositions of the present day. Each era has its own musical language, its own forms, its own technical demands, and its own emotional world. Understanding these eras — and the repertoire that defines them — is [essential](/blog/piano-jazz-standard-repertoire-list) for any pianist who wants to build a well-rounded musical education.
This guide maps the essential repertoire across five major eras, with recommendations for different skill levels within each. Whether you're a [beginner](/blog/piano-repertoire-guide-by-skill-level) looking for your first Baroque piece or an advanced player exploring contemporary repertoire, you'll find a roadmap here for building a balanced, historically informed collection of pieces.
## Baroque Era (1600–1750)
The Baroque era predates the modern piano — most keyboard music from this period was written for the harpsichord or clavichord. But these works translate beautifully to the modern piano and form the foundation of every serious pianist's education. Baroque music is characterized by counterpoint (multiple independent melodic lines), strict rhythmic patterns, and elaborate [ornamentation](/blog/piano-melody-ornamentation-techniques).
**Johann Sebastian Bach** is the towering figure of Baroque keyboard music. His **Two-Part Inventions** (BWV 772–786) are the gateway for most pianists into Baroque counterpoint — fifteen short pieces, each in a different key, that teach independent hand coordination and contrapuntal thinking. They're accessible to early-intermediate players and rewarding even for advanced pianists. The **Three-Part Inventions** (Sinfonias) are more challenging, requiring greater independence and control.
Bach's **Well-Tempered Clavier** is one of the most important works in all of keyboard literature. It consists of two books, each containing a prelude and fugue in every major and minor key — 48 pieces in total. The preludes range from simple to virtuosic; the fugues range from two to five voices. For intermediate players, the C major Prelude from Book I (BWV 846) is a beautiful and accessible starting point. Advanced players should explore the fugues, which are among the most intellectually demanding and emotionally profound pieces in the repertoire.
**Domenico Scarlatti** wrote over 500 keyboard sonatas — short, brilliant pieces that are technically demanding and full of Iberian flair. They're less studied than Bach but are treasures of the repertoire, with fast passages, hand crossings, and surprising harmonic turns. The Sonata in D minor, K. 32, and the Sonata in G major, K. 14, are accessible to intermediate players.
<Note>
**Ornamentation**: Baroque music includes ornaments — trills, mordents, turns — that are not fully written out. The performer is expected to realize these ornaments according to the conventions of the era. For beginners, start by learning the standard realizations and listening to recordings by historically informed performers.
</Note>
## [Classical](/blog/classical-piano-sheet-music-for-beginners) Era (1750–1820)
The Classical era is when the piano as we know it came into being. Composers wrote specifically for the new instrument, with its dynamic range and sustaining pedal, creating music that was clearer, more balanced, and more structurally transparent than Baroque music. The sonata form — with its exposition, development, and [recapitulation](/blog/piano-sonata-form-explained-simply) — became the dominant structure.
**Wolfgang Amadeus [Mozart](/blog/piano-duet-repertoire-for-four-hands)** wrote eighteen piano sonatas, which are cornerstones of the Classical repertoire. They demand clarity, evenness, and a light touch — the notes must sparkle. The Sonata in C major, K. 545 (the "Facile" sonata) is technically approachable for intermediate players but musically profound. The Sonata in A minor, K. 310, is darker and more dramatic, suitable for advanced players. Mozart also wrote beautiful variations and shorter pieces, including the beloved "Eine kleine Nachtmusik" arrangement.
**Ludwig van Beethoven** bridges the Classical and Romantic eras. His thirty-two piano sonatas are often called the "New Testament" of piano music (with Bach's Well-Tempered Clavier as the "Old Testament"). The early sonatas — Op. 2, Op. 10, Op. 13 ("Pathétique") — are accessible to intermediate and advanced players. The middle sonatas — Op. 27 No. 2 ("Moonlight"), Op. 53 ("Waldstein"), Op. 57 ("Appassionata") — push the instrument to its limits. The late sonatas — Op. 101, 106 ("Hammerklavier"), 109, 110, 111 — are among the most profound works in all of music.
**Muzio Clementi** is less famous than Mozart or Beethoven but is essential for developing pianists. His Sonatinas, Op. 36, are standard teaching pieces that introduce Classical sonata form in miniature. They're perfect for early-intermediate players building their first Classical repertoire.
## Romantic Era (1820–1900)
The Romantic era is the golden age of piano music, as we explored in our guide to [Romantic era composers](/blog/romantic-era-piano-composers-guide). The repertoire is vast, emotionally deep, and technically demanding. Key works include Chopin's Nocturnes, Études, and Preludes; Schumann's character pieces (Carnaval, Kinderszenen, Kreisleriana); Brahms' late Intermezzi; and Liszt's Hungarian Rhapsodies and Sonata in B minor.
For intermediate players, Chopin's Preludes, Op. 28, offer accessible gems — the E minor Prelude (No. 4) and the C major Prelude (No. 1) are beautiful and manageable. Schumann's "Träumerei" from Kinderszenen is a lyrical masterpiece within reach of intermediate players. Brahms' Intermezzo in A major, Op. 118 No. 2, is one of the most beloved short pieces in the Romantic repertoire.
Advanced players should explore the Chopin Ballades, the Liszt Sonata in B minor, the Brahms Sonata in F minor, and the Schumann Fantasie in C major. These are monumental works that represent the pinnacle of Romantic piano composition.
## 20th Century and Beyond
The twentieth century shattered the conventions of tonality, rhythm, and form. **Claude Debussy** created Impressionist piano music that prioritized color, atmosphere, and suggestion over traditional melody and harmony. "Clair de lune" from the Suite Bergamasque is his most famous piece — accessible to intermediate players and a gateway to his more advanced works like the Préludes and Images.
**Maurice Ravel** combined Impressionist color with Classical precision. His "Pavane pour une infante défunte" is a beautiful, accessible piece. "Gaspard de la nuit" is one of the most difficult piano pieces ever written. The Sonatine is an excellent intermediate-level work that balances lyricism with technical challenge.
**Contemporary composers** like Philip Glass, Steve Reich, Ludovico Einaudi, and Max Richter have created a new kind of piano music — minimalist, repetitive, and deeply emotional. Glass's "Metamorphosis" is accessible to intermediate players and has introduced countless people to contemporary classical music. Einaudi's "Una Mattina" and "I Giorni" are beloved by players and listeners alike.
## Building a Balanced Repertoire
A well-rounded pianist should have pieces from each era. This isn't just about breadth — each era teaches different skills. Baroque music teaches counterpoint and finger independence. Classical music teaches clarity and formal understanding. Romantic music teaches expression and tone production. Twentieth-century music teaches color and rhythm. Contemporary music teaches pattern and atmosphere.
When selecting pieces, match the repertoire to your skill level. Don't jump to advanced repertoire before you're ready — you'll develop tension and bad habits. Instead, choose pieces that are challenging but achievable, that stretch you without overwhelming you. A good teacher can help you find the right balance.
Finally, consider creating themed recital programs. A program of "night music" might include Bach's D minor Toccata, Chopin's Nocturne in Eb major, Debussy's "Clair de lune," and Einaudi's "Nightbook." A program of "dances" might include a Scarlatti sonata, a Chopin mazurka, a Brahms waltz, and a Glass étude. Themed programs give your repertoire a narrative shape and make practicing more engaging.
The piano repertoire is one of humanity's great artistic treasures. Three centuries of genius, written for an instrument that can whisper and roar, that can be played alone in a practice room or before thousands in a concert hall. Explore it widely, play it deeply, and let it become part of who you are.
