Jazz standards are the shared language of jazz musicians. These are the songs — mostly from the Great American Songbook and the bebop era — that every jazz player is expected to know, that are called at jam sessions, and that form the foundation of the [jazz repertoire](/blog/jazz-piano-sheet-music-recommendations). For pianists, learning the standards is both a rite of passage and a lifelong pursuit, because each one offers a unique harmonic and melodic world to explore.
This guide offers a curated list of essential standards, with context and tips for learning each one. Whether you are a jazz newcomer or an experienced player, these are the tunes that will serve you in any setting.
## What Makes a Jazz Standard
A jazz standard is a song that has become widely played and recognized in the jazz tradition. Most come from three sources:
- **The Great American Songbook** — popular songs by composers like George Gershwin, Cole Porter, Richard Rodgers, and Harold Arlen, written for Broadway and Hollywood between the 1920s and 1950s.
- **Bebop compositions** — tunes written by jazz composers like Charlie Parker, Dizzy Gillespie, and Thelonious Monk in the 1940s and 50s, often based on the chord progressions of earlier songs.
- **Modal and post-bop tunes** — compositions from the late 1950s onward by musicians like Miles Davis, John Coltrane, and Wayne Shorter.
A working jazz pianist is expected to know dozens of standards by memory, in multiple keys. Learning them is a gradual process that builds both repertoire and the harmonic understanding that makes improvisation possible.
<Callout type="info">
The definitive reference for jazz standards is the *Real Book*, a collection of lead sheets (melody and chords) for hundreds of tunes. Most jazz musicians learn from the Real Book, though many also learn tunes by ear from recordings.
</Callout>
## The Essential List
Here are twenty standards that form the core of the jazz piano repertoire, grouped by their primary characteristics.
### Ballads
| Standard | Composer | Key Feature |
|----------|----------|-------------|
| Autumn Leaves | Joseph Kosma | ii-V-I in major and minor — the essential practice tune |
| Body and Soul | Johnny Green | Rich harmony, rubato tradition |
| Misty | Erroll Garner | Beautiful melody, accessible harmony |
| My Funny Valentine | Richard Rodgers | Modal sections, expressive ballad |
| Round Midnight | Thelonious Monk | Complex harmony, iconic mood |
Ballads teach you to play melodically, to use space, and to navigate slow tempos with harmonic sophistication. "Autumn Leaves" in particular is the tune most jazz teachers use to introduce ii-V-I progressions in both major and minor keys.
### Medium Swing Tunes
| Standard | Composer | Key Feature |
|----------|----------|-------------|
| All the Things You Are | Jerome Kern | Modulates through multiple keys — a harmonic masterclass |
| Fly Me to the Moon | Bart Howard | Accessible, widely called |
| Just Friends | John Klenner | [Common](/blog/hanon-piano-exercises-practice-guide) turnaround, great for practice |
| Oleo | Sonny Rollins | Rhythm changes (based on Gershwin's "I Got Rhythm") |
| Satin Doll | Duke Ellington | Accessible, great for learning voicings |
These tunes are the workhorses of the jazz repertoire. "All the Things You Are" is particularly important — its harmonic movement through several keys makes it a benchmark of jazz literacy. "Oleo" introduces "rhythm changes," the second most common progression in jazz after the blues.
### Bossa Novas
| Standard | Composer | Key Feature |
|----------|----------|-------------|
| Blue Bossa | Kenny Dorham | Minor key, ii-V-I practice in a Latin style |
| Desafinado | Antonio Carlos Jobim | Beautiful melody, characteristic harmony |
| The Girl from Ipanema | Jobim | Iconic, widely known |
| Wave | Jobim | Great for practicing major ii-V-I |
Bossa novas offer a change of feel and a chance to work on Latin rhythms. For more on this style, see our [bossa nova piano patterns tutorial](/blog/bossa-nova-piano-patterns-tutorial). "Blue Bossa" is an essential first bossa for most pianists.
### Bebop and Post-Bop Tunes
| Standard | Composer | Key Feature |
|----------|----------|-------------|
| Blue Seven | Sonny Rollins | Blues form, great for melodic playing |
| Footprints | Wayne Shorter | Minor blues, 3/4 time, modern sound |
| Impressions | John Coltrane | Modal (Dorian), great for scale practice |
| So What | Miles Davis | Modal (Dorian), iconic, accessible |
| Song for My Father | Horace Silver | Latin-tinged hard bop, memorable melody |
Modal tunes like "So What" and "Impressions" are wonderful for beginners because they use few chords, allowing you to focus on melodic improvisation rather than rapid harmonic changes. For the theory behind this, see our [piano music theory for jazz players](/blog/piano-music-theory-for-jazz-players).
## How to Learn a Jazz Standard
Learning a standard is more than memorizing the chords. A thorough approach involves several layers:
1. **Learn the melody.** Memorize the melody so you can sing it and play it in any key. The melody is the soul of the tune.
2. **Learn the chord progression.** Understand the harmony, not just the chord symbols. Identify the ii-V-I progressions, turnarounds, and modulations.
3. **[Listen](/blog/piano-duet-repertoire-for-four-hands) to recordings.** Hear how great musicians have interpreted the tune. Each standard has a rich recorded history to draw on.
4. **Practice comping.** Play the chords with rhythmic style, supporting a soloist or yourself. Our guide to [jazz voicings for [intermediate](/blog/piano-repertoire-guide-by-skill-level) players](/blog/jazz-piano-voicings-for-intermediate-players) is a great resource here.
5. **Practice improvising.** Start by improvising just on the melody, then gradually add your own ideas over the chord changes.
6. **Transcribe solos.** Learning a great solo on a standard by ear is the deepest way to absorb its language.
## Playing in All Twelve Keys
A mark of a serious jazz musician is the ability to play standards in any key. Vocalists, horn players, and jam sessions often call tunes in keys other than the original. Practicing standards in multiple keys builds both transposition skills and deeper harmonic understanding.
Start with one standard — "Autumn Leaves" is ideal — and learn to play it in several keys. Use the chord relationships (the ii-V-I patterns) rather than memorizing each key separately.
## [Building Your Repertoire](/blog/piano-repertoire-for-small-hands) Over Time
Don't try to learn all these tunes at once. Aim to add one or two new standards to your repertoire each week or month, learning each thoroughly. Over a year, you will know dozens, and you will have built the harmonic vocabulary that makes learning new tunes easier and improvising more natural.
The standards are a shared heritage — a body of beautiful songs that have been reimagined by generations of musicians. Learning them connects you to that tradition and gives you the common language you need to play with other jazz musicians anywhere in the world. Start with one, listen deeply, and let the music teach you.
