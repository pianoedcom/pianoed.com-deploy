An acoustic piano is a complex mechanical instrument with thousands of moving parts, over 200 strings under tremendous tension, and a soundboard made of wood that breathes with the air around it. Treat it well, and it will reward you with beautiful music for decades — even generations. Neglect it, and it will fall out of tune, develop sticky keys, and lose the voice that made you fall in love with it in the first place.
## How Often Should You Tune Your Piano?
The standard recommendation is twice a year, ideally at the change of seasons. This schedule accounts for the humidity and temperature fluctuations that cause the piano's wood (soundboard, pinblock, bridges) to expand and contract, which changes string tension and throws the piano out of tune.
New pianos need more frequent tuning during their first year. The strings are still stretching and settling, and the piano may need three or four tunings before it stabilizes. This is normal — don't be alarmed if your new piano goes out of tune quickly at first.
**Signs your piano needs tuning** include: unison strings (two or three strings for the same note) that sound "wavy" or beat against each other, notes that sound sour or harsh, and a general sense that the piano sounds "off" even if you can't identify a specific problem. If you play a C major chord and it sounds like a C minor, you need a tuning.
## Climate and [Humidity Control](/blog/how-to-care-for-piano-keys-and-finish)
Humidity is the single biggest factor in piano stability. Wood expands in high humidity and contracts in low humidity, changing the tension on the strings and the alignment of the [action](/blog/upright-piano-vs-grand-piano-sound-difference). The ideal humidity for a piano is 42–55% relative humidity, maintained as consistently as possible.
A **Damp-Chaser** system (or similar climate control system) is one of the best investments you can make for an acoustic piano. These systems combine a humidifier and dehumidifier inside the piano, maintaining consistent humidity regardless of seasonal changes. They dramatically reduce the need for tuning and prevent the cracking and warping that humidity swings cause.
If you don't have a climate control system, use a room humidifier in winter (when heating dries the air) and a dehumidifier in summer. Keep the piano away from heating vents, air conditioning ducts, radiators, and direct sunlight. Even placing the piano on an inside wall (rather than an outside wall) helps with temperature stability.
## Cleaning and Care
**Cleaning keys** safely: use a slightly damp, soft cloth (microfiber works well). Don't use chemical cleaners, alcohol, or abrasive materials — they can damage the key surfaces. For ivory keys, a small amount of mild soap in water is acceptable. For plastic keys, just water is usually sufficient. Wipe each key individually from back to front, and dry immediately.
**Dusting the soundboard** is tricky because it's hard to reach. Use a soft brush attachment on a vacuum cleaner, or a long-handled duster designed for pianos. Don't use cloths or liquids inside the piano — you could damage the strings or soundboard.
**What to avoid:** never place drinks on the piano. Liquid spills inside a piano can cause catastrophic damage to the soundboard, strings, and action. Keep plants away — the humidity from watering can cause rust on strings. Don't place the piano near a window where sunlight can fade the finish and temperature swings are greatest.
## When to Call a Technician
**Sticking keys** are the most common mechanical problem. A key that doesn't return, or that returns slowly, usually has a felt or leather part that has swollen from humidity or worn with age. A technician can diagnose and fix this quickly — it's usually a simple adjustment.
**Pedal problems** — squeaking, rattling, or not functioning — are also common. The pedal mechanism has felt and leather parts that wear over time. A technician can replace or adjust these parts.
**Tuning instability** — when a piano won't stay in tune even after being tuned — can indicate a problem with the pinblock (the wood that holds the tuning pins). If the pinblock is cracked or dried out, the pins can't hold tension. This is a serious issue that requires professional assessment.
If your piano has been neglected for years, don't expect a single tuning to fix it. A piano that hasn't been tuned in five years may need a "pitch raise" — a rough tuning to bring it close to pitch, followed by a fine tuning after the strings settle. This process may need to be repeated over several months.
## Long-Term Piano Care
**Regulation** is the adjustment of the piano's action — the thousands of moving parts that connect key to hammer. Over time, felt compresses, springs weaken, and the action goes out of regulation. A well-regulated piano is easier to play, more responsive, and more expressive. Regulation should be done every 5–10 years, depending on use.
**Voicing** is the adjustment of the hammer felt to change the piano's tone. Hard hammers produce a bright, penetrating sound. Soft hammers produce a warm, mellow sound. A technician can voice your piano to match your tonal preference — making it brighter for a large hall or warmer for a small room.
**When to rebuild:** after decades of heavy use, a piano may need rebuilding — replacing strings, hammers, dampers, and sometimes the soundboard. This is expensive ($10,000–$30,000 for a grand) but can give a beloved instrument a new life. For high-[quality](/blog/acoustic-vs-digital-piano-comparison) pianos (Steinway, Bösendorfer, Yamaha SX series), rebuilding is often worth it. For inexpensive pianos, replacement is usually more economical.
**Protecting your investment:** treat your piano as you would a piece of fine furniture and a precision instrument simultaneously. Keep it clean, climate-controlled, and regularly serviced. A well-maintained piano can last 50–100 years and become a family heirloom. The care you give it today will be appreciated by the pianists who play it tomorrow.
