Your piano is just the beginning. The [accessories](/blog/best-digital-pianos-for-home-practice) you pair with it — pedals, stands, benches, metronomes, [headphones](/blog/best-piano-keyboards-for-travel) — can dramatically affect your playing experience. A great digital piano with a flimsy sustain pedal and an uncomfortable bench will leave you frustrated, no matter how good the instrument itself is. Conversely, the right accessories can elevate even a modest instrument, making practice more enjoyable and helping you develop proper technique.
This guide covers the accessories that matter most for [pianists](/blog/best-piano-for-small-apartment-spaces), with specific recommendations at different price points. Whether you're setting up your first home practice space or upgrading from the included accessories that came with your digital piano, you'll find practical advice here to help you make informed choices.
## Sustain Pedals: The [Essential](/blog/best-beginner-keyboard-piano-under-500) Accessory
The sustain pedal is the one accessory you absolutely cannot do without. It's also the one that most digital piano manufacturers cut corners on. The sustain pedal included with most entry-level digital pianos is a small, lightweight plastic switch that slides around the floor, offers no resistance, and gives you no tactile feedback. It's the piano equivalent of a laptop keyboard — functional, but far from optimal.
A quality sustain pedal replicates the feel of an acoustic piano pedal. It has weight (so it stays in place), resistance (so you can feel the pedal's position without looking), and a proper pivot mechanism (so the pedal moves naturally under your foot). The difference is immediately noticeable — your pedaling becomes more precise and expressive because you can actually feel what the pedal is doing.
Top aftermarket sustain pedals include the Yamaha FC3A (which supports half-pedaling on compatible instruments), the Roland DP-10 (sturdy and reliable with non-slip base), and the M-Audio SP-2 (a budget-friendly option that's still a massive upgrade over included pedals). If your digital piano supports half-pedaling — a technique where you partially depress the pedal to allow some damping while sustaining others — make sure the pedal you choose supports this feature.
<Callout>
**Half-pedaling explained**: On an acoustic piano, the sustain pedal doesn't simply turn damping on and off. Partial pedaling allows varying degrees of sustain, which is essential for nuanced playing. Digital pianos that support half-pedaling replicate this, but only if your pedal is capable of sending the intermediate positions. Check your piano's manual to see if this feature is supported.
</Callout>
## Expression and Una Corda Pedals
If you're playing on a digital piano that supports three-pedal units, you have access to the soft (una corda) and sostenuto pedals in addition to the sustain pedal. The soft pedal on an acoustic grand shifts the entire [action](/blog/midi-piano-controllers-for-home-studio) slightly to the right, so the hammers strike fewer strings, producing a softer, more muted tone. On digital pianos, this is simulated by reducing the volume and slightly altering the tone.
The sostenuto pedal sustains only the notes that are already being held when the pedal is pressed. New notes played after the pedal is engaged are not sustained. This pedal is used in advanced repertoire to sustain certain notes or chords while playing staccato passages in the same hand. It's a subtle but powerful tool that many pianists never learn to use effectively.
Three-pedal units for digital pianos come in two forms: built-in (integrated into a furniture-style stand) and standalone (a separate unit that connects to your piano). Built-in units are more stable and feel more like an acoustic piano, but they lock you into one position. Standalone units are more flexible but can be less stable. If you're serious about developing advanced pedaling technique, a three-pedal unit is worth the investment.
## Stands, Mounts, and Furniture
If you have a portable digital piano or stage piano, you need a stand. The two main types are X-stands and furniture stands. X-stands are lightweight, portable, and adjustable in height. They're ideal for gigging musicians or anyone who needs to set up and tear down regularly. However, they can be less stable than furniture stands, and the crossbar can interfere with your legs when pedaling.
Furniture stands are designed to stay in one place. They're more stable, they look better in a home environment, and they often include a three-pedal unit mounted in the correct position. If your digital piano is going to live in one spot in your home, a furniture stand is the better choice. Most manufacturers make furniture stands specifically designed for their instruments, ensuring a perfect fit.
Wall mounting is an option for some compact digital pianos. This saves floor space and can look striking in a modern interior, but it requires careful installation and limits your ability to adjust the instrument's position. If you go this route, make sure the mount is rated for the weight of your instrument and that you're mounting into wall studs, not just drywall.
<Note>
**Stability matters**: A wobbly stand is more than just annoying — it can affect your playing. If the piano shifts when you play forcefully or pedal energetically, your technique will suffer. Invest in a stand that's solid enough to stay put, no matter how enthusiastically you play.
</Note>
## Metronomes and Practice Aids
A metronome is your most important practice tool. It provides the steady tempo reference that you need to develop rhythmic accuracy and build speed gradually. While there are many metronome options available — from mechanical pendulum metronomes to smartphone apps — the best metronome is the one you'll actually use consistently.
[Mechanical metronomes](/blog/best-piano-metronomes-and-practice-tools) have a visual appeal that many pianists love. The swinging pendulum provides a visual rhythm reference in addition to the audible click, and the wooden case looks beautiful on a piano. Wittner and Seiko are the leading brands, with models ranging from $40 to $150. The downside is that they're not as feature-rich as digital alternatives, and the volume can be difficult to adjust.
Digital metronomes offer [features](/blog/best-stage-pianos-for-live-performance) that mechanical ones can't match: multiple time signatures, accent beats, tap tempo, and volume control. Dedicated digital metronomes like the Korg MA-2 or Boss DB-30 are compact, reliable, and affordable ($20–$40). They're loud enough to hear over your playing and small enough to sit on the piano without getting in the way.
Metronome apps are the most convenient option since you always have your phone with you. Apps like Pro Metronome, Tempo, and Soundbrenner offer features that go far beyond basic timekeeping — polyrhythms, setlists, visual flashes, and even vibration feedback (with a smartwatch). The downside is that your phone is also a source of distraction, so discipline is required to avoid checking messages during practice.
## Headphones and Audio Accessories
If you practice on a digital piano, good headphones are essential for late-night practice and for hearing the details of your playing. The right headphones reveal nuances in the piano's sound that you might miss through the built-in speakers — sympathetic resonance, damper noise, and the decay characteristics of different registers.
Open-back headphones like the Audio-Technica ATH-M40x or Beyerdynamic DT 770 Pro provide a natural, spacious sound that's ideal for evaluating piano tone. Closed-back headphones offer better isolation (important if you're in a noisy environment) but can make the sound feel more confined. For piano practice, either type works well — choose based on your environment and personal preference.
If you want to record your playing, a simple audio interface like the Focusrite Scarlett Solo ($100) lets you connect your digital piano's audio output to your computer for high-quality recording. A pair of studio monitors — like the PreSonus Eris E3.5 ($100) — can also improve your practice experience by giving you better [sound quality](/blog/acoustic-vs-digital-piano-comparison) than the built-in speakers on most digital pianos.
Don't forget the basics: a good quality instrument cable (if your piano uses 1/4" outputs), a headphone extension cable (so you're not tethered to the piano), and a cable organizer to keep your setup tidy. These small investments make your practice space more functional and more pleasant to use, and they remove the small frictions that can make practice feel like a chore.
