The Baroque era — roughly 1600 to 1750 — was a time of extraordinary musical innovation. It gave us opera, the concerto, the fugue, and the sonata. It gave us Bach, Handel, Vivaldi, and Scarlatti. And for keyboard players, it was the era that defined the instrument's role in Western music. The keyboard went from being a functional tool for accompaniment and theory to becoming a solo instrument capable of profound musical expression.
When we talk about Baroque keyboard music, we're talking about music written for the harpsichord, the clavichord, and the earliest pianos. [The modern piano](/blog/history-of-the-piano-evolution) as we know it didn't exist yet — it was being invented during this period, and its development was influenced by the musical demands of Baroque composers. Understanding Baroque keyboard music is essential for any pianist, not only because it forms the foundation of the keyboard repertoire, but because the musical principles of the era — counterpoint, harmony, structure — underpin everything that came after.
## The Keyboard Instruments of the Baroque
Three keyboard instruments dominated the Baroque era, each with its own character and capabilities. Understanding these instruments helps us understand the music written for them.
**The harpsichord** was the most important keyboard instrument of the Baroque. Its mechanism is fundamentally [different](/blog/romantic-era-piano-composers-guide) from the piano: instead of hammers striking strings, the harpsichord uses plectra (originally quills, later plastic) that pluck the strings. This means the volume is determined by the registration (which sets of strings are engaged) rather than by how hard you press the keys. You cannot play louder or softer by pressing harder — the harpsichord has a fixed dynamic level for each registration. To create dynamic contrast, harpsichordists use two manuals (keyboards) with different registrations and switch between them, or they add and remove stops.
The harpsichord's sound is bright, clear, and rich in overtones. It's perfect for the contrapuntal music of the Baroque, where each voice needs to be distinct and audible. The instrument's inability to change dynamics through touch also shaped the music written for it — Baroque composers created dynamic contrast through registration changes, terraced dynamics (sudden shifts between loud and soft), and changes in texture and register rather than through gradual crescendos and diminuendos.
**The clavichord** was the intimate, expressive cousin of the harpsichord. Its mechanism is simple: a tangent (a small brass blade) strikes the string and remains in contact with it as long as the key is held. This allows the player to control dynamics through touch — press harder and the note is louder; press softer and it's quieter. The clavichord can even produce a vibrato-like effect (Bebung) by varying pressure on the key after the note is struck. But the clavichord is extremely quiet — it was a practice instrument, played alone in a small room, not in concert halls.
**The early piano** was invented around 1700 by [Bartolomeo Cristofori](/blog/piano-in-baroque-era-history), a harpsichord maker in Florence. Cristofori's ingenious mechanism used hammers that struck the strings and then fell back, allowing the string to vibrate freely. This meant the player could control dynamics through touch — the fundamental innovation that defined the piano. Cristofori called his instrument the "gravicembalo col piano e forte" (harpsichord with soft and loud), which was eventually shortened to "pianoforte" and then simply "piano." The early piano was a remarkable instrument, but it took decades to catch on — most Baroque composers wrote for the harpsichord, which was the established instrument.
## J.S. Bach and the Keyboard Tradition
Johann Sebastian Bach (1685-1750) is the towering figure of Baroque keyboard music. His output for keyboard instruments is staggering in its quantity, quality, and musical depth. Bach wrote for the harpsichord, the clavichord, and — in his later years — the early piano. He may also have written some of his works for the organ, which shares the keyboard but has a completely different mechanism.
**Bach's keyboard works and their significance**: The Well-Tempered Clavier (two books of preludes and fugues in every major and minor key) is perhaps the most important keyboard work ever written. It was conceived as a demonstration of the possibilities of well-tempered tuning, which allows playing in all keys without some sounding badly out of tune. The Inventions and Sinfonias are two- and three-part inventions that Bach wrote for his students, teaching them to play cleanly and expressively in multiple voices. The English and French Suites are collections of dance movements that demonstrate Bach's mastery of the suite form. The Goldberg Variations are a monumental set of variations on a simple aria, showcasing every keyboard technique of the era.
**The Well-Tempered Clavier and tuning**: The tuning system that Bach demonstrated in the Well-Tempered Clavier was revolutionary. Before well-tempered tuning, keyboard instruments were tuned so that keys with few sharps and flats sounded good, but keys with many sharps and flats sounded badly out of tune. Well-tempered tuning (and later equal temperament) distributed the tuning compromises evenly across all keys, making every key playable. This opened up the entire harmonic universe of the keyboard — composers could modulate to distant keys without fear of unplayable dissonance.
**Bach's influence on future keyboard music**: Bach's keyboard music set the standard for contrapuntal writing, harmonic sophistication, and structural integrity. Every major composer who followed — Mozart, Beethoven, [Chopin](/blog/great-piano-competitions-history-guide), Debussy — studied Bach's keyboard works. The Well-Tempered Clavier has been called the "Old Testament" of the piano repertoire (with Beethoven's sonatas as the "New Testament"). Bach's fusion of technical mastery with deep musical expression created a model that has inspired pianists for three centuries.
## Handel, Scarlatti, and Other Baroque [Masters](/blog/famous-classical-pianists-who-changed-music)
While Bach is the most revered Baroque keyboard composer, he was not alone. Several other composers made extraordinary contributions to the keyboard repertoire.
**Handel's keyboard suites**: George Frideric Handel (1685-1759) is best known for his operas and oratorios, but he also wrote significant keyboard music. His eight Great Suites (published in 1720) are the most important of his keyboard works. They contain a mixture of dance movements and free pieces, showing Handel's gift for melody and dramatic contrast. Handel's keyboard music is less contrapuntally complex than Bach's but more immediately accessible, with memorable tunes and clear structures.
**Scarlatti's sonatas and innovation**: Domenico Scarlatti (1685-1757) is one of the most fascinating figures in keyboard music. He wrote over 550 keyboard sonatas — short, single-movement pieces that are astonishing in their variety and invention. Scarlatti worked at the Portuguese and Spanish courts, and his music reflects Iberian influences: guitar-like effects, folk dance rhythms, and bold harmonic excursions. His sonatas are technically demanding, featuring rapid hand crossings, wide leaps, and unusual figurations. They're a treasure trove for pianists — each one is a miniature masterpiece of keyboard writing.
**French and English keyboard schools**: In France, the Couperin family — especially François Couperin (1668-1733) — wrote elegant, ornamental harpsichord music in the French style. Couperin's pieces have descriptive titles and are organized into ordres (suites) that create a narrative or atmospheric progression. In England, Henry Purcell (1659-1695) wrote keyboard music in a more austere style, while the Italian-born Muzio Clementi (1752-1832) straddled the Baroque and [Classical](/blog/best-piano-recordings-of-all-time) eras, writing sonatas that bridged the two styles.
## Baroque Keyboard Style and Techniques
Baroque keyboard music has a distinctive style that reflects the instruments and aesthetic of the era. Understanding this style is essential for playing Baroque music on the modern piano.
**Counterpoint and fugue**: Baroque music is fundamentally contrapuntal — it consists of multiple independent melodic lines (voices) that interact with each other. The fugue is the supreme expression of contrapuntal writing, with a subject (theme) introduced in one voice and then imitated in others. Playing counterpoint requires the ability to hear and shape each voice independently, bringing out entries of the subject while keeping other voices in proportion.
**Ornamentation in Baroque music**: Baroque music is heavily ornamented — composers expected performers to add trills, mordents, turns, and other ornaments to the written notes. Ornamentation was partially improvised, and different national styles had different conventions. Bach and other composers often wrote out ornaments explicitly, but performers were expected to add more. On the modern piano, ornamentation requires careful study of historical practice and tasteful execution.
**Basso continuo and its role**: In much Baroque music, the keyboard player serves as the harmonic foundation, reading a figured bass (a bass line with numbers indicating chords) and improvising the accompaniment. This practice, called basso continuo or thoroughbass, was central to Baroque music-making. The keyboard player was both accompanist and improviser, creating the harmonic framework in real time.
## Playing Baroque Music on the Modern Piano
Playing Baroque music on the modern piano requires thoughtful adaptation. The piano's capabilities — dynamic control through touch, sustained tone, and the pedal — are different from the harpsichord's, and Baroque music was written with the harpsichord's characteristics in mind.
**Adapting harpsichord music for piano**: The piano can do things the harpsichord cannot — play crescendos and diminuendos, sustain notes with the pedal, and produce a singing tone. Use these capabilities tastefully. Dynamic contrast in Baroque music should generally follow the musical structure (terraced dynamics between sections, dynamic shaping within phrases) rather than imposing Romantic-style expressiveness.
**Pedaling considerations for Baroque works**: Use the pedal sparingly in Baroque music. The pedal blurs the clarity of contrapuntal voices, which is essential to Baroque style. Use it only for legato in slow movements or for resonance in chordal passages. In fast contrapuntal movements, play without pedal to maintain clarity. When in doubt, use less pedal rather than more.
**[Recommended](/blog/piano-concerto-history-guide) Baroque pieces for pianists**: Start with Bach's Two-Part Inventions — they're accessible, beautiful, and teach essential contrapuntal skills. Then move to the preludes and fugues of the Well-Tempered Clavier (the C Major prelude from Book 1 is a wonderful starting point). Explore Scarlatti's sonatas for their brilliance and variety. Try Handel's suites for their melodic charm. And don't neglect the French repertoire — Couperin's pieces are exquisite miniatures that teach delicate touch and ornamentation.
Baroque keyboard music is not a museum piece — it's living music that speaks as powerfully today as it did three centuries ago. The principles it teaches — clarity, structure, counterpoint, and expressive restraint — are relevant to every pianist, regardless of the repertoire they play. By engaging with Baroque music, you connect with the roots of the keyboard tradition and deepen your understanding of everything that came after.
