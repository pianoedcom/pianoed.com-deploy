Jazz piano sheet music is a different world from classical. In classical music, you play what's written. In jazz, the written music is a starting point — a sketch that you bring to life through interpretation, improvisation, and your own musical personality. Understanding the resources available will transform your jazz piano journey.
## Understanding Fake Books and Real Books
A "fake book" is a collection of lead sheets — simplified notations showing the melody, chord symbols, and lyrics of a song. The name comes from the idea that you can "fake" your way through a song using just this minimal information. Fake books are the working musician's bible.
The **Real Book** is the most famous jazz fake book. Originally an illegal, hand-copied collection compiled by Berklee students in the 1970s, it's now published legally by Hal Leonard in multiple volumes. The Real Book contains hundreds of jazz standards with accurate melodies and chord changes. If you play jazz, you need a Real Book.
The **New Real Book** series by Sher Music offers higher-quality engraving, more accurate transcriptions, and a wider selection of modern jazz. Many professional musicians prefer these editions for their accuracy and readability.
How to use a fake book effectively: don't just play the melody and chords as written. Use the lead sheet as a framework — experiment with different chord voicings, add fills and embellishments, and develop your own arrangement. The goal isn't to reproduce a standard version but to make it your own.
## [Essential Jazz](/blog/piano-jazz-standard-repertoire-list) Piano Collections
**For beginners:** "Jazz Piano Voicings for the Non-Pianist" by Mike Tracy teaches the essential left-hand voicings (rootless voicings, shell voicings) that every jazz pianist needs. "Intro to Jazz Piano" by Mark Harrison is a method book that takes you from basic chord theory to playing real standards.
**For [intermediate](/blog/piano-repertoire-guide-by-skill-level) players:** "The Jazz Piano Book" by Mark Levine is the definitive reference. It covers everything from basic voicings to advanced reharmonization, with examples from the masters. Every jazz pianist should own this book. "Jazz Piano Harmony" by Phil DeGreg is a structured approach to voicings and comping.
**For advanced players:** "The Harmony of Bill Evans" by Jack Reilly analyzes the harmonic language of one of jazz's most innovative pianists. "Metaphors for the Musician" by Randy Halberstadt covers advanced concepts in improvisation and comping.
## Learning from Transcriptions
Transcriptions — notated versions of recorded solos — are invaluable for understanding how jazz pianists think. By studying and playing transcriptions, you internalize the vocabulary, phrasing, and harmonic choices of the masters.
Famous transcribed solos every jazz pianist should study: Bill Evans' solo on "Autumn Leaves" (from the album "Portrait in Jazz"), Bud Powell's solo on "Un Poco Loco," and Oscar Peterson's solo on "Hymn to Freedom." These are available in published transcription books and online.
How to study transcriptions: don't just learn to play them — analyze them. What scales is the pianist using? How do they connect chords? What rhythmic devices do they employ? The goal isn't to copy but to understand the thinking behind the notes.
## Lead Sheets and Chord Symbols
Reading chord changes is a fundamental jazz skill. A lead sheet might show "Cm7 — F7 — Bbmaj7 — Ebmaj7" — four chord symbols that tell you the harmony but leave the voicing, rhythm, and voice leading up to you.
Common jazz notation includes: triangle (△) for major 7, dash (-) for minor, circle (°) for diminished, and slash (/) for half-diminished. Extensions like 9, 11, and 13 add color tones. Alterations like b5, #5, b9, #9 change the character of the chord.
Creating your own voicings is the next step. Start with shell voicings (root + 3rd or 7th), then add extensions (9ths, 11ths, 13ths), then explore rootless voicings that leave the bass to the bass player and give you more freedom in the upper [structure](/blog/best-piano-method-books-compared).
## [Building](/blog/classical-piano-sheet-music-for-beginners) a Jazz Repertoire
**Must-know standards:** "Autumn Leaves," "All The Things You Are," "Blue Bossa," "Take Five," "My Funny Valentine," "Fly Me to the Moon." These are called at jam sessions worldwide. Learn them in multiple keys.
**Blues forms:** The 12-bar blues is the foundation of jazz. Learn it in Bb, F, and Eb (the most common keys). Study the differences between basic blues, jazz blues, and minor blues.
**Bebop heads for study:** "Ornithology" (Charlie Parker), "Donna Lee" (Charlie Parker), "Oleo" (Sonny Rollins). These melodic lines are studies in bebop language — playing them builds your vocabulary for improvisation.
The beauty of jazz piano is that the same song is never played the same way twice. Your sheet music is a map, not a script. Learn it, then let it go.
