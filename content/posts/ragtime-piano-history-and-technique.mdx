Before jazz, before the blues became a national phenomenon, before rock and roll was even a glimmer, there was ragtime. It was America's first truly original piano music — a fusion of African rhythmic traditions and European march forms that swept the nation at the turn of the twentieth [century](/blog/evolution-of-piano-design-through-centuries). And at its center was a man named Scott Joplin, whose compositions remain as delightful to play today as they were a hundred years ago.
Ragtime is fun. That's something that can't be said about every piano genre. It's upbeat, syncopated, and rhythmically infectious. When you play a rag, your foot taps, your head nods, and a smile spreads across your face. But beneath the surface fun lies genuine technical challenge and musical sophistication. Ragtime requires independence of hands, rhythmic precision, and a touch that is both precise and relaxed. It's the perfect genre for pianists who want to bridge the gap between [classical](/blog/best-piano-recordings-of-all-time) technique and popular music.
## The Birth of Ragtime
Ragtime emerged in the 1890s in the African American communities of the American Midwest, particularly in St. Louis and Sedalia, Missouri. It grew from the cakewalk — a dance popular in minstrel shows — and the march music of John Philip Sousa. The crucial innovation was **syncopation**: the right hand played melodies that emphasized the off-beats, while the left hand maintained a steady march-like rhythm. This "ragged" rhythm — the source of the name "ragtime" — was unlike anything in European music.
**Scott Joplin** (1868–1917) was the King of Ragtime. A classically trained pianist and composer, Joplin elevated ragtime from a popular entertainment to a genuine art form. His compositions — "Maple Leaf Rag" (1899), "The Entertainer" (1902), "Pineapple Rag" (1908), and many others — are masterpieces of the genre, combining sophisticated harmonic language with irresistible rhythmic energy.
Joplin had ambitions beyond ragtime. He composed two operas and a ballet, believing that ragtime could be the foundation for a serious American classical music. While his operas were not successful in his lifetime, his ragtime compositions became enormously popular, selling millions of copies of sheet music. "Maple Leaf Rag" was the first instrumental piece to sell over one million copies of sheet music — a staggering achievement.
Ragtime's influence on jazz and popular music was enormous. The syncopated rhythms of ragtime directly influenced early jazz, and the left-hand patterns of ragtime evolved into the stride piano style of the 1920s and 30s. Elements of ragtime can be heard in everything from the playing of Fats Waller to the compositions of William Bolcom. When you learn ragtime, you're learning the roots of American popular music.
<Callout>
**Historical Note**: Joplin was adamant that ragtime should not be played fast. He wrote on his sheet music: "Notice! Don't play this piece fast. It is never right to play Ragtime fast." Respect the composer's wishes — a moderate tempo that allows the syncopation to be heard clearly is far more effective than a rushed performance.

## The Left-Hand Stride Pattern
The foundation of ragtime piano is the left-hand **stride** pattern, sometimes called the "oom-pah" pattern. The left hand alternates between low bass notes (the "oom") and mid-range chords (the "pah"), playing one on each beat. In 2/4 time, this means: beat 1 — low bass note, beat 2 — chord, beat 3 — low bass note, beat 4 — chord. The bass notes are typically the root of the chord on beats 1 and 3, and the fifth (or sometimes the root an octave up) on beats 2 and 4.
This pattern is harder than it looks. The left hand must cover a wide range — jumping from the bottom of the keyboard to the middle and back — while maintaining a steady, even rhythm. The jumps must be accurate (no wrong notes) and the rhythm must be rock-steady (no rushing or dragging). This requires a combination of spatial awareness, muscle memory, and relaxed arm movement.
To develop this technique, start slowly. Play the left-hand pattern alone, at a tempo of 60 beats per minute or slower. Focus on accuracy — make sure every bass note and chord is exactly where it should be. Then gradually increase the tempo, but never faster than you can play cleanly. Speed comes from accuracy, not from effort.
The key to the stride pattern is the **release**. After playing the low bass note, the hand must release quickly and move to the chord position. The tendency is to hold the bass note too long, which delays the chord and disrupts the rhythm. Practice releasing the bass note immediately after playing it, letting the hand float to the chord position without tension.
## Right-Hand Syncopation
While the left hand maintains the steady stride pattern, the right hand plays **syncopated melodies** that emphasize the off-beats. The melody notes fall on the "and" of each beat — the spaces between the left hand's steady pulse. This creates the characteristic "ragged" feeling that gives the genre its name.
The syncopation takes many forms. Sometimes the right hand plays a melody note on the off-beat while the left hand plays a bass note on the beat. Sometimes the right hand plays a chord on the off-beat, creating a rhythmic accent that clashes with the left hand's steady pulse. The interplay between the steady left hand and the syncopated right hand is what creates the infectious rhythmic energy of ragtime.
**Typical melodic patterns** in ragtime include repeated notes, grace notes, and short chromatic runs. Joplin's melodies often feature a distinctive three-note pattern: a main note, its upper neighbor, and a return to the main note. This "turn" figure, played in syncopation, is one of the signatures of the ragtime style.
Coordinating the syncopated right hand with the steady left hand is the central challenge of ragtime piano. The two hands are playing different rhythms, and the brain must process them independently. This is why ragtime is such excellent training for hand independence — it forces you to develop the ability to separate the hands rhythmically.
## Essential Ragtime Pieces to Learn
**"Maple Leaf Rag"** is the cornerstone of the ragtime repertoire. It was Joplin's first major hit and remains his most performed piece. It's technically demanding — the left-hand jumps are wide, the right-hand syncopation is complex, and the tempo must be steady throughout. But it's also incredibly rewarding to play. Start with the first section (the A section) and learn it thoroughly before moving on.
**"The Entertainer"** is more accessible for beginners. Made famous by the 1973 film "The Sting," it's one of the most recognizable melodies in American music. The sections are shorter and the technical demands are slightly less than "Maple Leaf Rag," making it a good first ragtime piece for intermediate pianists. The famous introduction and first section can be learned relatively quickly, though the later sections present more challenge.
**"Pineapple Rag"** and **"Fig Leaf Rag"** offer variety and are less frequently played than the two giants above. "Pineapple Rag" has a lyrical, almost romantic B section that provides contrast to the energetic outer sections. "Fig Leaf Rag" features a particularly catchy first section and a challenging second section. Both are excellent choices for pianists who have mastered "The Entertainer" and want to expand their ragtime repertoire.
For non-Joplin rags, explore the works of **James Scott** ("Frog Legs Rag") and **Joseph Lamb** ("Sensation Rag"). These three [composers](/blog/piano-in-film-and-popular-culture) — Joplin, Scott, and Lamb — are known as the "Big Three" of ragtime, and their works represent the pinnacle of the genre.
## Ragtime's [Legacy](/blog/history-of-the-piano-evolution) and Modern Revival
Ragtime experienced a massive revival in the 1970s, triggered largely by the success of "The Sting" (1973), which featured several Joplin rags on its soundtrack. "The Entertainer" became a pop hit, reaching #3 on the Billboard Hot 100 — a remarkable achievement for a piano piece written seventy years earlier. The revival introduced ragtime to a new generation of pianists and listeners, and Joplin's complete works were recorded and published in scholarly editions.
The revival also sparked new composition in the ragtime style. **William Bolcom** wrote "Graceful Ghost Rag" and other neo-ragtime pieces that combine traditional ragtime forms with modern harmonic language. **Max Morath** performed and lectured on ragtime, bringing the genre to audiences across America. Today, ragtime festivals are held annually in Sedalia, Missouri, and other cities, keeping the [tradition](/blog/baroque-keyboard-music-history-guide) alive.
Ragtime's influence extends far beyond its own era. The stride piano of James P. Johnson and Fats Waller grew directly from ragtime. The left-hand patterns of boogie-woogie have their roots in ragtime's bass-chord alternation. Even the syncopated rhythms of rock and pop music can trace their lineage back to the ragged rhythms of Scott Joplin.
When you play ragtime, you're not just playing old music — you're participating in a living tradition. The joy of ragtime is timeless: the toe-tapping rhythm, the clever syncopation, the satisfaction of coordinating two independent hands. Scott Joplin gave America its first great piano music, and it remains as vital and delightful today as it was when it first filled the [parlors](/blog/piano-cultural-impact-on-modern-music) and saloons of a century ago.
