One of the great advantages of a digital piano over an acoustic is [connectivity](/blog/silent-piano-systems-comparison). Where an acoustic piano is a self-contained instrument, a digital piano can talk to computers, phones, tablets, amplifiers, recording interfaces, and other instruments. This opens up recording, learning apps, notation software, virtual instruments, and live performance tools. But the array of ports and protocols — USB, MIDI, Bluetooth, audio — can be confusing. This guide explains each connection, what it's for, and how to choose the right setup.
## The Two Kinds of Connection: Data vs Audio
Before diving into ports, it helps to understand that piano connections fall into two categories:
- **Data connections (MIDI)** — carry performance information: which notes you played, how hard, how long, which pedals you pressed. No sound, just instructions. A computer can use this data to play virtual instruments, record your performance, or drive notation software.
- **Audio connections** — carry the actual sound of the piano. These connect your piano to speakers, amplifiers, headphones, or recording devices.
<Callout type="note">
A common confusion: MIDI carries no sound. If you connect a MIDI cable to a speaker, you'll hear nothing. MIDI tells another device what to play; audio is what you actually hear.
</Callout>
Most modern setups use both — MIDI to record or control software, audio to hear the result.
## MIDI Connections
MIDI (Musical Instrument Digital Interface) is the universal language of digital music. It has been around since the 1980s and remains the backbone of digital piano [connectivity](/blog/midi-piano-controllers-for-home-studio).
### Traditional 5-Pin DIN MIDI
The classic MIDI ports: MIDI In, MIDI Out, and sometimes MIDI Thru, using round 5-pin connectors. These carry only data. They're found on most stage pianos and professional instruments, and they connect to MIDI interfaces, other keyboards, and hardware synths.
- **MIDI In** — receives MIDI data into the piano (e.g., to play the piano's internal sounds from another controller)
- **MIDI Out** — sends the piano's MIDI data to another device
- **MIDI Thru** — passes through the MIDI In data unchanged, for chaining devices
### USB to Host (USB MIDI)
Most modern digital pianos include a USB port labeled "USB to Host" or "USB MIDI." This carries MIDI data over a single USB cable to a computer, tablet, or phone. It's simpler than 5-pin DIN — one cable, no interface needed — and is the standard for connecting to [recording software](/blog/piano-recording-setup-for-home-studio), learning apps, and notation programs.
USB to Host is the connection most home players will use day to day.
### USB to Device
Some pianos add a "USB to Device" port — a standard USB port for flash drives. This lets you record your performance as MIDI or audio directly to a USB stick, or load songs and styles onto the piano. It's a handy, computer-free way to capture playing.
## Bluetooth MIDI
Newer digital pianos increasingly offer Bluetooth MIDI, which sends MIDI data wirelessly to computers, tablets, and phones. This eliminates cables entirely for MIDI — ideal for connecting to learning apps on an iPad or a phone without dongles.
- **Pros:** No cables, easy pairing, great for tablets and phones
- **Cons:** Slight latency on some devices, requires a Bluetooth-capable piano and receiving device, can be less stable than wired connections for critical recording
<Callout type="tip">
Bluetooth MIDI is excellent for practice and learning apps, but for serious recording with tight timing, a wired USB MIDI connection is more reliable and lower-latency.
</Callout>
## Audio Connections
Audio connections carry the piano's sound to the outside world.
### Headphone Output
Nearly every digital piano has a headphone jack — usually 1/4-inch or 3.5mm, sometimes both. This is the simplest audio connection and essential for silent practice. Some pianos have two headphone jacks for teacher and student.
### Line Out (Left/Right)
For connecting to external speakers, amplifiers, mixers, or recording interfaces, digital pianos provide line outputs — usually a pair (left and right) of 1/4-inch jacks. These carry the piano's sound at a fixed or adjustable level suitable for amplification or recording.
For [live performance](/blog/best-stage-pianos-for-live-performance) and recording, line outputs are the primary connection.
### USB Audio
Some modern pianos send audio over the same USB cable that carries MIDI — a single connection handles both data and sound to a computer. This is convenient for home recording, since one cable to your computer carries both your performance data and the resulting audio.
### Bluetooth Audio
A few pianos can receive Bluetooth audio — letting you stream music from a phone through the piano's speakers for play-along practice. This is a playback feature, not a recording connection.
## [Choosing](/blog/best-piano-apps-for-learning-and-practice) Cables and Adapters
- **USB to Host:** usually a USB Type-B to USB Type-A or Type-C cable (printer-style), depending on the piano
- **5-pin MIDI:** standard MIDI cables, or a USB-MIDI interface for connecting to a computer
- **Audio:** 1/4-inch TS or TRS cables for line out; 1/4-inch or 3.5mm for headphones
- **Tablet/phone:** often requires a USB adapter (Lightning, USB-C, or a camera adapter) to connect a USB to Host cable
Check your piano's manual for the exact cable types — they vary by model.
## Common Setups
### Home Practice with Learning Apps
- **Connection:** USB to Host or Bluetooth MIDI to a tablet running a learning app
- **Audio:** headphones or the piano's built-in speakers
- **Why:** MIDI data drives the app's feedback; you hear the piano's own sound
### Home Recording
- **Connection:** USB to Host (MIDI) to a computer running a DAW, plus USB audio or line out for sound
- **Why:** MIDI captures your performance for editing; audio captures the actual tone
### Live Performance
- **Connection:** Line out (left/right) to a mixer or amplifier; USB to Host or 5-pin MIDI to control other synths or software
- **Why:** Line out feeds the PA; MIDI lets the piano drive additional sounds
### Playing Virtual Instruments
- **Connection:** USB to Host (MIDI) to a computer running a [piano VST](/blog/best-piano-vst-plugins-for-producers) or other virtual instrument
- **Audio:** the computer's audio output to speakers or headphones
- **Why:** The piano acts as a controller; the computer generates the sound
## Troubleshooting Common Issues
- **No sound from MIDI:** MIDI carries no audio — connect audio separately or use a virtual instrument on the receiving device
- **Computer doesn't recognize the piano:** install any required drivers, use the correct USB cable (data, not charge-only), and select the piano as the MIDI input in your software
- **Bluetooth won't pair:** ensure both devices support Bluetooth MIDI, turn Bluetooth off and on, and move closer during pairing
- **Latency (delay):** use wired USB MIDI for recording; reduce buffer size in your DAW; avoid Bluetooth for timing-critical work
- **Hum or noise on audio:** use balanced cables if available, check grounding, and keep audio cables away from power cables
## The [Future](/blog/new-piano-technology-innovations) of Connectivity
Connectivity is one of the fastest-evolving areas of digital pianos. Expect more pianos to offer single-cable USB audio+MIDI, reliable Bluetooth MIDI, and tighter integration with learning platforms. When shopping, the connectivity a piano offers is as important as its sound and action — it determines how the instrument grows with your needs.
Understanding your piano's connections turns it from a standalone instrument into the center of a flexible musical setup. Match the connection to the task, use [quality](/blog/acoustic-vs-digital-piano-comparison) cables, and your digital piano will connect to whatever you need it to.
