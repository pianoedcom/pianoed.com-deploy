The piano pedal is one of the most misunderstood components of the instrument. Most pianists know that pressing the right pedal makes the sound "ring out," but far fewer understand the distinct function of each pedal, how they work mechanically, and when to use them for different musical styles. This knowledge gap is unfortunate, because the pedals are powerful expressive tools that can dramatically shape the color, resonance, and character of your playing.
This guide explains every type of piano pedal — the three standard pedals on acoustic pianos, the variations found on digital pianos, and the techniques for using each one effectively.
## The Three Standard Piano Pedals
### Sustain Pedal — The Most Used Pedal
The sustain pedal, also called the damper pedal, is the rightmost pedal and the one most pianists use most frequently. When you press it, all the dampers lift off the strings simultaneously, allowing them to vibrate freely. This creates the rich, resonant sound that most people associate with piano music.
The sustain pedal does more than simply hold notes — it also activates sympathetic resonance. When the dampers are lifted, strings that you haven't played begin to vibrate in sympathy with the notes you have played, adding richness and depth to the sound. This sympathetic resonance is what gives a well-pedaled piano its warm, singing quality.
The sustain pedal is used for legato playing (connecting notes that can't be held by the fingers alone), for creating a wash of harmonic color, and for sustaining [chords](/blog/how-to-play-piano-chords-for-beginners) beyond what the hands can hold. It is the most expressive of the three pedals and the one that requires the most skill to use well.
### Soft Pedal (Una Corda) — Tone Color Change
The soft pedal, also called the una corda pedal, is the leftmost pedal. Despite its name, its primary function is not simply to make the piano quieter — it changes the tone color of the instrument.
On a grand piano, the una corda pedal shifts the entire keyboard and action slightly to the right. This causes the hammers to strike fewer strings — typically two instead of three for most notes, or one instead of two for the lowest notes. The result is not just a quieter sound, but a different tonal color: softer, more muted, and slightly veiled.
On an upright piano, the soft pedal works differently. Instead of shifting the action, it moves the hammers closer to the strings, reducing the distance they travel and producing a quieter sound. The tonal change is less dramatic on an upright than on a grand, but the softening effect is still useful.
<Callout>
**The una corda is a color pedal, not just a volume pedal.** Use it when you want a different tonal quality — something more intimate, distant, or veiled. Simply playing more softly with your fingers produces a different effect than using the una corda.
</Callout>
### Sostenuto Pedal — Selective Sustain
The sostenuto pedal is the middle pedal on a grand piano, and it's the least understood of the three. Its function is selective sustain: it sustains only the notes that are already being held down when you press it, while all subsequent notes are played normally (with their dampers functioning).
This allows you to hold a bass note or chord while playing staccato or articulated notes above it — something that would be impossible with the sustain pedal, which would hold all notes. The sostenuto is particularly useful in advanced [repertoire](/blog/how-to-play-piano-with-small-hands) where you need to sustain a low note while playing detached chords or [melodies](/blog/how-to-play-piano-by-ear) in the upper registers.
On many upright pianos, the middle pedal does something different — it functions as a "practice pedal" that lowers a piece of felt between the hammers and strings, dramatically reducing the volume for quiet practice. Some digital pianos omit the sostenuto pedal entirely or assign a different function to the middle pedal.
## How Each Pedal Works Mechanically
### Damper Mechanism in Grand Pianos
In a grand piano, each note has its own damper — a small felt-covered block that rests on the strings to stop them from vibrating. When you press a key, the damper lifts off the strings for that note, allowing it to sound. When you release the key, the damper returns to the strings, stopping the sound.
When you press the sustain pedal, a mechanism lifts all the dampers simultaneously, regardless of which keys are pressed. This is why all notes continue to ring when the pedal is held — all the dampers are up.
The damper mechanism is one of the most complex parts of the piano action, and its precise regulation (adjustment) is critical to the piano's responsiveness. A poorly regulated damper system can cause notes to ring when they shouldn't or fail to stop cleanly when keys are released.
### Una Corda Action Shift Explained
In a grand piano, the una corda pedal is connected to the keyboard and action by a mechanism that shifts the entire assembly to the right. The shift distance is small — typically about 2-3 millimeters — but it's enough to move the hammers from striking three strings to striking two (or from two to one for the lowest notes).
The shift mechanism must be precisely adjusted. If the shift is too small, the hammers still strike all three strings and the tonal change is minimal. If the shift is too large, the hammers may strike the wrong strings or miss them entirely.
### Sostenuto Mechanism and Its Limitations on Uprights
The sostenuto mechanism in a grand piano uses a series of tabs that engage with the damper levers. When you hold down a key (lifting its damper) and then press the sostenuto pedal, a tab catches that damper lever, [holding](/blog/how-to-build-piano-finger-[independence](/blog/best-piano-finger-exercises-for-beginners)) it up even after you release the key. Other dampers, whose keys were not held down when the pedal was pressed, are not engaged and function normally.
Upright pianos typically do not have a true sostenuto mechanism. The middle pedal on most uprights either operates a practice mute (felt strip) or, in some cases, sustains only the bass notes. If you need true sostenuto functionality, you need a grand piano or a high-end digital piano that simulates it.
<Note>
**Not all middle pedals are created equal.** Before relying on the sostenuto pedal in [performance](/blog/how-to-memorize-piano-pieces-faster), test it on the specific piano you'll be playing. Some pianos have a true sostenuto, others have a bass-sustain, and others have a practice mute. Knowing which type you're dealing with prevents surprises on stage.
</Note>
## Pedals on Digital Pianos
### Single Pedal vs Triple Pedal Units
Entry-level digital pianos often come with a single sustain pedal — a simple on/off switch that mimics the basic function of the sustain pedal. This is sufficient for beginners but limits the player's expressive options.
Mid-range and professional digital pianos typically include a triple pedal unit that replicates all three pedals of an acoustic piano: sustain, soft, and sostenuto. These pedal units are often built into the piano's stand or available as separate accessories.
### Continuous vs On/Off Pedal Detection
A critical difference in digital piano pedals is whether they detect continuous position or simply on/off. Basic sustain pedals are binary — they're either up or down. More advanced pedals detect the full range of motion, allowing for half-pedaling and other nuanced techniques.
Continuous pedal detection is essential for advanced pedaling techniques. Without it, you cannot perform half-pedaling, quarter-pedaling, or gradual pedal changes — all of which are important for expressive playing in classical and romantic repertoire. If you're serious about piano technique, choose a digital piano with continuous pedal detection.
### Assignable Pedals and Their Functions
Some digital pianos allow you to assign different functions to the pedals. Common assignments include:
- **Sustain**: The standard function
- **Sostenuto**: Selective sustain, as on an acoustic piano
- **Soft/Una corda**: Tone color change
- **Pitch bend/modulation**: For synth sounds
- **Style start/stop**: For arranger keyboards
- **Layer/dual**: Toggle a second instrument sound
If your digital piano has assignable pedals, check the manual to understand the options and choose the assignments that best suit your playing style.
## Pedaling Techniques for Different Styles
### Legato Pedaling for Classical Music
In classical music (Mozart, Haydn, early Beethoven), pedaling is typically light and minimal. The pedal is used to connect notes that the fingers can't hold, not to create a wash of sound. Change the pedal with each harmonic change, and use "clean" pedaling — lifting the pedal fully between changes to avoid blurring.
### Syncopated Pedaling for Romantic Repertoire
In romantic music (Chopin, Liszt, Schumann, Debussy), syncopated (delayed) pedaling is the standard approach. Press the pedal a fraction of a second after playing the note, and change the pedal at the exact moment of each harmonic change. This creates a smooth, singing legato while maintaining harmonic clarity.
### Minimal Pedaling for Jazz and Pop
In jazz and pop piano, pedaling is generally used more sparingly than in classical music. Jazz pianists often avoid the pedal entirely, relying on finger legato and sustain from the natural resonance of the instrument. When the pedal is used, it's typically for brief moments — a single chord sustain or a short passage — rather than continuous pedaling.
<Callout>
**When in doubt, pedal less.** The most common pedaling mistake is over-pedaling — holding the pedal down too long, creating a muddy, indistinct sound. If you're not sure whether to pedal, try playing without it first. You can always add pedal, but it's harder to remove the habit of over-pedaling.
</Callout>
## Common Pedaling Questions Answered
### When NOT to Use the Pedal
There are many situations where the pedal should not be used:
- **Baroque music**: Bach, Scarlatti, and other baroque composers wrote for instruments without sustain pedals. While some pedaling is acceptable on the modern piano, it should be minimal.
- **Fast, articulated passages**: In fast passages where clarity is essential, the pedal can blur the notes and should be avoided or used very sparingly.
- **Staccato passages**: By definition, staccato requires notes to be short and detached. The pedal sustains notes, working against the staccato effect.
- **When practicing**: Practice without the pedal to ensure you can play the passage cleanly with your fingers alone. Add the pedal only after the passage is secure.
### How to Pedal in Fast Passages
In fast passages, the pedal should be used selectively and briefly. "Flutter pedaling" — rapidly pressing and releasing the pedal — can add resonance without blurring the notes. This technique is used in fast scale passages, arpeggios, and other virtuosic passages where some sustain is desired but clarity must be maintained.
### Adapting Pedaling to Different Pianos and Rooms
The same pedaling approach will sound different on different pianos and in different rooms. A bright, lively room may require less pedal than a dead, dry room. A piano with a long sustain may need less pedal than one with a short sustain. Always listen to the acoustic environment and adjust your pedaling accordingly. What works on your practice piano at home may need adjustment when you perform on a different instrument in a different space.
Understanding piano pedals is essential for expressive, musical playing. Each pedal has a distinct function and character, and knowing when and how to use them — and when not to — is one of the marks of a mature pianist. Take the time to explore each pedal's capabilities, and your playing will gain a new dimension of color, depth, and expression.
