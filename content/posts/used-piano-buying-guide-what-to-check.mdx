Buying a used piano can be one of the smartest decisions a pianist makes. A well-maintained [acoustic](/blog/acoustic-vs-digital-piano-comparison) piano can last for decades, and the depreciation on new pianos is steep — a piano that cost $10,000 new might sell for $4,000 five years later. But a used piano can also be a money pit disguised as a bargain. Cracked soundboards, loose tuning pins, and worn actions can turn a "great deal" into a financial nightmare.
The key to buying a used piano is knowing [what to look for](/blog/piano-bench-buying-guide-for-proper-posture). You don't need to be a piano technician to spot the major red flags — you just need to know where to look and what questions to ask. This guide will walk you through every part of the inspection process, from the first phone call to the final negotiation.
## Why Buy Used? The [Value Proposition](/blog/best-hybrid-pianos-reviewed)
New pianos lose a significant portion of their value the moment they're delivered, much like new cars. A piano that costs $8,000 in a showroom might be worth $5,000 the next day. This steep depreciation means that the [used market](/blog/best-beginner-keyboard-piano-under-500) is where the best values are found. A piano that's five to ten years old, if well-maintained, offers the same playing experience as a new instrument at a fraction of the cost.
Vintage instruments offer something new pianos can't: character. Pianos from the golden age of American piano manufacturing (roughly 1900–1940) were built with old-growth wood and craftsmanship that is rare and expensive today. A restored Steinway from the 1920s can sound and feel better than a brand-new one — and cost less. Similarly, certain [European](/blog/best-piano-brands-comparison-guide) pianos from the mid-twentieth century have tonal qualities that modern instruments struggle to replicate.
For beginners and students, a used piano is almost always the smarter choice. The most important thing for a beginner is having a real acoustic instrument with weighted keys and a good action — not the prestige of a new instrument. A used Yamaha or Kawai [upright](/blog/upright-piano-vs-grand-piano-sound-difference) in good condition will serve a student for years and cost half the price of a new one.
<Callout>
**Rule of Thumb**: If a used piano costs less than 50% of its new equivalent price and passes a technician's inspection, it's generally a good deal. If it costs more than 70% of new price, you're better off buying new — unless it's a vintage or rare instrument.
</Callout>
## Inspecting the Soundboard and Strings
The soundboard is the large wooden panel inside the piano that amplifies the vibration of the strings. It's the heart of the instrument's acoustic system, and damage to it can be catastrophic. Open the piano and look at the soundboard — it should be a single, continuous piece of wood (or a few joined pieces) without visible cracks.
Cracks in the soundboard are not always deal-breakers, but they need evaluation. Hairline cracks that don't affect the sound may be acceptable, especially in older pianos. However, cracks that run along the grain and are accompanied by separation from the ribs underneath can cause buzzing and reduce the instrument's resonance. If you see significant cracks, have a technician assess whether they affect the sound.
The strings should be checked for rust and wear. Light surface tarnish is normal and harmless, but heavy rust indicates the piano has been stored in a humid environment, which can also damage the pinblock and action. Look at the bass strings (the copper-wound strings on the left) — if the copper is green or heavily tarnished, the strings may need replacing, which costs several hundred dollars.
Tuning pin tightness is critical. The tuning pins are the metal pegs around which the strings are wound. If they're loose, the piano won't hold a tune and may need an expensive repair (replacing the pinblock or oversizing the pins). You can test this yourself: if the piano is badly out of tune in specific notes, or if the tuner has recently told the owner that the piano "won't hold a tune," the pins may be loose. A technician can test pin torque during an inspection.
## Action and Key Examination
The action is the mechanical system that transmits the movement of the keys to the hammers that strike the strings. It's the most complex part of a piano and the most expensive to repair. Testing the action is one of the most important parts of a used piano inspection.
Play every key on the piano, from the lowest to the highest. Listen for evenness of tone and volume. Feel for consistency in key resistance — every key should require roughly the same amount of force to press. Keys that are noticeably harder or easier to press than others may need regulation, which is a time-consuming and costly process.
Check for sticky or sluggish keys. Press each key slowly and release it — it should return to its resting position immediately and silently. If a key sticks down, returns slowly, or makes a clicking or squeaking sound, there's a problem in the action. Minor issues (a felt bushing that needs replacing) are inexpensive to fix, but widespread action problems can cost more than the piano is worth.
Examine the hammers. Remove the music desk and look down at the hammers — the felt-covered heads that strike the strings. Deep grooves in the felt indicate heavy use. Light grooves can be fixed by reshaping the hammers (a moderate expense), but deeply grooved hammers may need replacement. Also check for moth damage — if the felt looks eaten or frayed, the hammers may need attention.
## Cabinet, Finish, and Structural Integrity
Cosmetic issues are generally not a concern unless they affect the structural integrity of the piano. Scratches and dings in the finish are cosmetic and can be refinished if desired, but they don't affect the sound. What matters is the structural condition of the case and frame.
Look for signs of moisture damage: warped wood, water stains, mold, or a musty smell. Pianos that have been in floods or stored in damp basements may have hidden damage to the soundboard, pinblock, and action. If you see any signs of water damage, walk away — the cost of repairing the damage will almost always exceed the value of the piano.
Check the pedal assembly. Press each pedal and listen for squeaks or resistance. The pedals should move smoothly and return to their resting position immediately. Pedal problems are usually inexpensive to fix, but they can indicate broader neglect if the piano hasn't been maintained.
The lid and fallboard (the cover that drops down over the keys) should open and close smoothly. If the fallboard sticks or the lid is warped, the piano may have been exposed to temperature and [humidity](/blog/piano-maintenance-and-tuning-guide) fluctuations. While these are minor issues, they suggest the piano may not have been well-cared-for.
## Negototiating and Final Steps
Before purchasing any used piano, have a registered piano technician inspect it. This costs $100–$200 and is the best insurance you can buy. A technician can identify issues you can't see — loose tuning pins, soundboard cracks, action problems — and give you a written assessment of the piano's condition and estimated repair costs. If the seller won't allow a technician inspection, walk away.
[Budget](/blog/best-digital-pianos-for-home-practice) for immediate costs beyond the purchase price. Every used piano should be tuned after moving (moving destabilizes the tuning), and it may need a second tuning a few weeks later as it adjusts to its new environment. Budget $200–$400 for tuning in the first few months. If the technician identifies any repairs, factor those into your offer.
Transportation is a significant cost that many buyers overlook. Professional piano moving costs $200–$600 depending on distance and difficulty (stairs, tight turns, etc.). Never move an acoustic piano yourself or with generic movers — the instrument can be damaged and you can be injured. Professional piano movers have the equipment and expertise to move safely.
When negotiating, use the technician's report as leverage. Any issue the technician identifies — even minor ones — is grounds for a price reduction. Be respectful but firm, and be prepared to walk away. There are always more pianos for sale, and the patience to wait for the right one will save you money and frustration in the long run.
A used piano, bought wisely, can be a lifelong companion. The piano you buy today could be the one your children learn on, the one you play for decades, the one that becomes part of your family's story. Take the time to choose well — the music you make will be worth it.
