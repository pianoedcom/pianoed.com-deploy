Recording your piano playing at home has never been more accessible. Whether you want to track your practice progress, share performances online, create content for students, or produce professional-quality recordings, a well-planned home studio setup can deliver remarkable results without breaking the bank. The key is understanding what equipment you actually need and how to use it effectively.
This guide walks you through everything from capturing an acoustic grand piano with microphones to recording a [digital](/blog/best-digital-pianos-for-home-practice) piano through an audio interface, along with the software that ties it all together.
## Why Record Your Piano Playing
Recording serves multiple purposes beyond simply capturing a performance. For practice, recording yourself reveals details you might miss in the moment — unevenness in your playing, pedaling issues, or tempo fluctuations that your ears filter out while you're focused on the mechanics of playing. Listening back to recordings from months ago also provides a powerful motivational boost when you hear how far you've come.
For teachers and content creators, recordings become shareable assets. A well-captured performance can be posted to YouTube, shared with students as reference material, or used as content for a piano blog or social media channel. The ability to produce clean, professional-sounding recordings from home opens doors to teaching online, collaborating remotely with other musicians, and building an audience around your playing.
<Callout>
**Start simple.** You don't need a thousand-dollar setup to begin recording. A smartphone recording captures more than enough detail to evaluate your practice. As your needs grow, invest in better equipment incrementally.
</Callout>
## Recording Acoustic Pianos
Capturing an acoustic piano is one of the most rewarding — and challenging — recording tasks. The piano is a complex sound source with a wide frequency range, dynamic envelope, and intricate overtones that shift depending on where you place your microphones.
### Microphone Types
Condenser microphones are the standard choice for piano recording. Their sensitivity and wide frequency response capture the full tonal spectrum of the instrument. Large-diaphragm condensers provide a warm, full-bodied sound that works well for solo piano, while small-diaphragm (pencil) condensers offer a more detailed, transparent capture that excels in ensemble settings.
Dynamic microphones are less sensitive but can handle high sound pressure levels, making them useful for close-miking the soundboard or for recording in untreated rooms where ambient noise is a concern. Ribbon microphones offer a smooth, vintage character that some engineers love for jazz and classical piano, though they are more fragile and expensive.
### Stereo Miking Techniques
For a natural, immersive piano sound, stereo miking is [essential](/blog/best-piano-for-small-apartment-spaces). The three most common techniques are:
- **AB (spaced pair)**: Two omnidirectional or cardioid microphones placed 40-60 cm apart, positioned 30-50 cm above the open lid. This produces a wide, spacious sound with strong low-frequency response.
- **XY (coincident pair)**: Two cardioid microphones angled at 90 degrees with their capsules as close together as possible. This creates a narrow, focused stereo image with excellent mono compatibility.
- **ORTF**: Two cardioid microphones angled at 110 degrees with 17 cm between capsules. This offers a natural, balanced stereo image that many engineers consider ideal for solo piano.
<Note>
Room acoustics matter as much as microphone choice. A room with hard, reflective surfaces will produce a bright, reverberant sound. Soft furnishings, rugs, and acoustic panels tame reflections and give you a cleaner, more controlled recording.
</Note>
## Recording Digital Pianos
Recording a digital piano is significantly simpler than recording an acoustic one, since the sound is already in electronic form. The two main approaches are audio recording and MIDI recording.
### [USB Audio](/blog/digital-piano-[connectivity](/blog/midi-piano-controllers-for-home-studio)-options-guide) Interface vs Direct [Line Out](/blog/digital-piano-[connectivity](/blog/silent-piano-systems-comparison)-options-guide)
Most digital pianos have a line output or headphone jack that can be connected directly to an audio interface. This gives you a clean, noise-free signal that captures exactly what the piano produces. A USB audio interface converts the analog line signal to digital audio that your computer can record.
Some modern digital pianos have built-in USB audio interfaces, allowing you to connect directly to your computer via USB cable without a separate interface. This simplifies the setup but may offer less control over gain staging and monitoring.
### MIDI Recording vs Audio Recording
MIDI recording captures the performance data — which keys you pressed, how hard, and for how long — rather than the actual sound. This gives you enormous flexibility: you can change the piano sound after recording by swapping VST plugins, correct mistakes by editing the MIDI data, and layer multiple instrument sounds from a single performance.
Audio recording captures the final sound as you hear it, which is simpler but less flexible. For most home studio purposes, recording both MIDI and audio simultaneously gives you the best of both worlds — the flexibility of MIDI editing with the security of a committed audio capture.
## Software and DAWs for Piano Recording
A Digital Audio Workstation (DAW) is the software that records, edits, and mixes your audio. The good news is that several excellent DAWs are free or very affordable.
### Free and Budget DAW Options
Reaper offers a full-featured 60-day trial and an affordable license. Audacity is free and handles basic stereo recording well, though it lacks MIDI support. GarageBand comes free with Mac and is surprisingly capable for piano recording. For Windows users, Cakewalk by BandLab is a fully featured DAW available at no cost.
### Piano-Specific VST Plugins
A VST (Virtual Studio [Technology](/blog/new-piano-technology-innovations)) piano plugin replaces your digital piano's built-in sounds with high-quality sampled or modeled piano sounds. Popular options include Pianoteq (physical modeling), Keyscape (extensive sampling), and the free Spitfire LABS piano. These plugins run inside your DAW and are triggered by MIDI data from your keyboard.
### Basic Mixing — EQ, Compression, Reverb
A few simple mixing techniques dramatically improve your piano recordings. Gentle EQ can reduce muddiness (cut around 200-300 Hz) or add clarity (a small boost around 5-8 kHz). Light compression evens out dynamic peaks, while reverb adds spatial depth and can make a dry, close-miked recording sound like it was captured in a concert hall.
## Building Your Setup on Any Budget
### Entry-Level Recording Under $200
A USB microphone like the Audio-Technica AT2020 USB+ or Blue Yeti can capture an acoustic piano adequately for practice recording and online sharing. For digital pianos, a simple 1/4-inch to USB cable or a basic interface like the Behringer UMC22 gets you started. Pair with Reaper or Audacity.
### Mid-Range Setup $200-600
Invest in a dedicated audio interface (Focusrite Scarlett 2i2 or MOTU M2), a pair of small-diaphragm condenser microphones (Rode M5 or sE Electronics sE7), and microphone stands. This setup delivers clean, professional-sounding stereo recordings of acoustic pianos and handles digital piano recording with ease.
### Professional Home Studio $600+
For serious recording, consider a higher-end interface (Universal Audio Apollo Solo or RME Babyface), premium microphones (Neumann KM 184 or AKG C414), acoustic treatment for your room, and professional VST plugins. At this level, your home recordings can rival commercial studio quality.
<Callout>
**The most important investment is your room.** A $200 microphone in a well-treated room will always sound better than a $2,000 microphone in an untreated, reflective space. Start with acoustic treatment before upgrading microphones.
</Callout>
Building a home piano recording setup is a journey of incremental improvements. Start with what you can afford, learn to use it well, and upgrade only when you can identify a specific limitation in your current setup. The best recording equipment is the equipment you actually know how to use.
