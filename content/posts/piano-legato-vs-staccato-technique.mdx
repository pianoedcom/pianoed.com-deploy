The piano can't sustain a note the way a violin or a singer can — once a key is struck, the sound begins to fade. What gives piano music its singing quality or its crisp bite is articulation: how we connect or separate notes. The two foundational articulations are legato (smooth and connected) and staccato (short and detached). Mastering both gives you control over the character of everything you play.
These aren't just technical labels — they're expressive tools. The same melody played legato sounds tender and lyrical; played staccato, it sounds playful or pointed. Understanding the physical difference is the first step to using articulation musically.
## What Legato and Staccato Mean
- **Legato** — notes are connected, with no gap between them; the next note begins as the previous one releases. Indicated by a slur or the word *legato*.
- **Staccato** — notes are short and detached, with a clear silence between them. Indicated by dots above or below the notes.
<Callout type="note">
On the piano, true legato is an illusion. Because each note decays, we create the impression of connection through timing, touch, and pedaling. The art is in making that illusion convincing.
</Callout>
## Playing Legato
Legato requires careful [finger](/blog/best-piano-finger-exercises-for-beginners) technique. The goal is to minimize the gap between notes so the melody sounds continuous.
- **Overlap slightly** — release the previous key just after (not before) the next one sounds
- **Transfer weight** — feel the weight of your arm move from one finger to the next
- **Keep fingers close to the keys** — high finger lifts create gaps; stay near the key surface
- **Listen for gaps** — if you hear silence between notes, you're releasing too early
### When Legato Is Hard
Legato is most challenging when the hand has to move (crossing fingers, large leaps) or when the melody is in the same hand as other notes. In those cases, the sustain pedal can help bridge gaps, but it should support, not replace, finger legato.
## Playing Staccato
Staccato is about brevity and clarity. The note should sound immediately and release quickly.
- **Flick the key** — a quick, light motion, as if the key is hot
- **Release immediately** — the silence after the note is part of the articulation
- **Keep it light** — staccato played heavily sounds harsh; aim for a crisp, bounced sound
- **Use the wrist** — a slight wrist bounce produces a more natural staccato than finger-only jabbing
There are degrees of staccato: *staccatissimo* (very short), standard staccato, and *mezzo staccato* (moderately detached). The shorter the note, the lighter the touch.
## The Physical Difference
The key technical distinction is in how you release the key:
- **Legato** — release slowly and late, overlapping with the next note
- **Staccato** — release instantly, creating a deliberate silence
This release timing is what your hand must learn. Practice alternating between the two on a single note until the contrast is obvious to your ear.
## Practice Exercises
### Exercise 1: One-Note Contrast
Play a single note (say, middle C) four times legato, then four times staccato. Focus purely on the release. Hear the difference.
### Exercise 2: Five-Finger Legato
Play C–D–E–F–G legato, aiming for a seamless line. Then play the same notes staccato, aiming for crisp separation. Alternate until both feel automatic.
### Exercise 3: Scale Articulation
Play a one-octave scale entirely legato, then entirely staccato. Legato scales reveal finger-overlap control; staccato scales reveal even, light touch.
## Using Articulation Expressively
Articulation isn't just technique — it's interpretation. Consider how it shapes character:
- **Legato** — lyrical [melodies](/blog/how-to-play-piano-by-ear), tender passages, singing lines
- **Staccato** — playful themes, rhythmic drive, light accompaniment
- **Mixed** — a lyrical right hand over a staccato left hand creates depth and contrast
<Callout type="tip">
When you learn a piece, decide the articulation deliberately. Don't default to whatever your hand finds easy — choose what serves the music.
</Callout>
## Common Pitfalls
- **Gappy legato** — releasing too early creates a chopped sound; overlap the notes
- **Heavy staccato** — pressing hard makes staccato harsh; keep it light and bounced
- **Same articulation everywhere** — uniformity flattens character; vary it intentionally
- **Ignoring the score** — articulation marks are the composer's instructions, not suggestions
Legato and staccato are the two poles of piano touch, and most playing lives somewhere between them. Learn to produce each cleanly, then explore the shades in between. Your control over articulation is a big part of what makes your playing sound intentional, expressive, and uniquely yours.
