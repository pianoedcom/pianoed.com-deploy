Ornaments are the flourishes that decorate a melody — a quick trill, a graceful turn, a delicate mordent. They add sparkle, character, and historical flavor to piano music, especially in [Baroque and [Classical repertoire](/blog/classical-piano-repertoire-by-era-guide)](/blog/piano-repertoire-for-small-hands). To many beginners they look like mysterious symbols, but each ornament follows a clear pattern. Once you know them, you can interpret them with confidence and add genuine elegance to your playing.
Ornamentation is part technique, part historical understanding, and part taste. Here's a practical guide to the most common ornaments.
## What Ornaments Are For
Ornaments decorate a main note without changing the essential melody. They were especially important in the Baroque and Classical eras, when performers were expected to embellish melodies tastefully. Today, composers notate them with symbols, and your job is to realize them as the style intends.
- **Add brilliance** — a trill shimmers above a sustained note
- **Create forward motion** — turns and grace notes push the line onward
- **Express historical style** — Baroque ornaments differ from Romantic ones
- **Show personality** — tasteful ornamentation reveals a performer's musicianship
<Callout type="note">
Ornaments aren't optional decorations you add on a whim — when notated, they're the composer's instructions. But how you realize them (speed, timing) involves informed taste.
</Callout>
## [The Trill](/blog/piano-melody-ornamentation-techniques)
A trill is the rapid alternation between a main note and the note above it (usually a whole or half step). It's indicated by "tr" above the note, often with a wavy line extending over the notes to be trilled.
- **Start on the upper note** in Baroque and Classical style (historically standard), though later styles often start on the main note
- **Alternate quickly and evenly** — the hallmark of a good trill is evenness, not raw speed
- **End with a turn** — many trills conclude with a little two-note flourish resolving to the main note
- **Use relaxed arm rotation** — never tense; speed comes from release, not effort
<Callout type="tip">
If your trill is uneven, slow it down. Evenness at slow speed becomes evenness at speed. A fast, sloppy trill is worse than a slow, clean one.
</Callout>
## The Mordent
A mordent is a single quick alternation between the main note and the note above (upper mordent) or below (lower mordent).
- **Upper mordent** (short wavy line): main note → upper neighbor → main note, quickly
- **Lower mordent** (wavy line with a line through it): main note → lower neighbor → main note
- **Play it crisply** — it's a quick flutter, not a sustained trill
The mordent is a brief, snappy ornament that adds a touch of color to a single note.
## The Turn
A turn is a four-note figure that "turns around" the main note: upper neighbor → main note → lower neighbor → main note. It's notated with a sideways "S" symbol above or between notes.
- **Above the note**: play the turn before the main note's full value
- **Between two notes**: the turn connects them, starting on the upper neighbor of the first note
- **Adjust for key** — the neighbors follow the key signature (sharps or flats apply)
Turns add graceful motion and are common in Classical-era melodies.
## Grace Notes
A grace note is a small note printed just before a main note. It's played quickly, leading into the main note.
- **Acciaccatura** (with a slash through the stem): played very quickly, almost crushed into the main note
- **Appoggiatura** (no slash): held longer, often taking half the value of the main note, creating a gentle leaning emphasis
- **On the beat** — most grace notes in classical style are played on the beat, just before the main note
The distinction between acciaccatura and appoggiatura matters in earlier music; in much Romantic and modern music, grace notes are simply played quickly.
## Glissando and Slide
While not ornaments in the strict sense, quick slides between notes (glissando) appear as flourishes. On the piano, a glissando is a rapid drag across the keys — a dramatic effect covered in its own technique.
## Historical Style Matters
Ornament realization depends on the era:
- **Baroque** — trills often start on the upper note; ornaments are more numerous and specific
- **Classical** — ornaments are lighter and more standardized; turns and trills are common
- **Romantic** — fewer notated ornaments; trills often start on the main note
- **Contemporary** — ornaments are usually written out in full notes rather than symbolized
When in doubt, consult an edition with editorial guidance or a style guide for the period.
## How to Practice Ornaments
1. **Learn the pattern slowly** — write out the actual notes the ornament produces
2. **Practice evenly** — the ornament's notes should be perfectly even
3. **Start slow, build speed** — only speed up when it's clean
4. **Integrate into the phrase** — the ornament should flow, not stick out
5. **Listen for musicality** — an ornament should enhance, not distract
## [Common [Mistakes](/blog/piano-articulation-marks-guide)](/blog/hanon-piano-exercises-practice-guide)
- **Playing ornaments too fast and unevenly** — speed without evenness sounds sloppy
- **Wrong starting note** — especially trills starting on the wrong note for the style
- **Ignoring the key signature** — neighbors must follow the sharps and flats of the key
- **Over-ornamenting** — when in doubt, simpler is often more elegant
Ornaments are a delightful layer of piano music — the difference between a plain melody and one that sparkles. Learn the symbols, understand the patterns, practice them evenly, and respect the historical style. With time, you'll add ornaments naturally, and the music you love from the great eras will come alive under your fingers with all its intended grace.
