The glissando is one of the most dramatic and visually impressive techniques on the piano. That sweeping slide across the keyboard, producing a cascade of notes in rapid succession, can be thrilling to hear and exhilarating to play. But glissandos are also technically demanding — they require specific hand positions, careful [finger](/blog/best-piano-finger-exercises-for-beginners) technique, and an understanding of how to produce speed without injury.
Whether you're encountering glissandos in a Chopin waltz, a Gershwin prelude, or a Ravel piece, this guide will teach you the proper technique for playing them safely, quickly, and beautifully.
## What Is a Glissando
### Definition and Types of Glissandos
A glissando (from the French "glisser," meaning "to slide") is a rapid slide across a series of adjacent notes on the piano. On the piano, a glissando produces a continuous cascade of sound as the finger or thumb slides across consecutive white or black keys.
There are two main types of piano glissandos:
- **White-key glissando**: Sliding across the white keys only, producing a diatonic scale pattern
- **Black-key glissando**: Sliding across the black keys only, producing a pentatonic pattern
Glissandos can be played ascending (low to high) or descending (high to low), with either the right or left hand, and they can span any number of octaves from a single octave to the entire keyboard.
### Glissando vs Portamento
It's worth noting that the term "glissando" is sometimes confused with "portamento." In string and vocal music, a portamento is a continuous pitch slide between two notes. On the piano, which produces discrete pitches, a true portamento is impossible. What pianists call a glissando is technically a rapid scale played with a sliding motion — discrete notes that sound nearly continuous due to their speed.
### Where Glissandos Appear in [Repertoire](/blog/how-to-build-piano-finger-independence)
Glissandos appear in a wide range of piano repertoire. In classical music, they're found in works by Chopin (the Waltz in E major, Op. 18), Liszt (numerous operatic paraphrases), and Ravel (Alborada del Gracioso). In the 20th century, Gershwin made extensive use of glissandos in works like Rhapsody in Blue and the Piano Preludes. Glissandos also appear in popular piano music, particularly in boogie-woogie and rock piano styles.
<Callout>
**A glissando is not just a technical effect — it's a musical gesture.** Think of it as a dramatic sweep of sound that connects two points in the music. The speed, dynamics, and character of the glissando should serve the musical context, not just showcase technique.
</Callout>
## White-Key Glissando Technique
### Using the Thumbnail for Descending Glissandos
For a descending white-key glissando (right hand, high to low), the most common technique uses the thumbnail. Turn your hand so that the thumbnail is in contact with the keys, and the rest of your hand is above the keyboard. The thumbnail acts as a "runner" that slides across the keys, depressing each one in rapid succession.
The key to a clean descending glissando is keeping the thumbnail at a consistent angle and pressure. If the angle is too shallow, the thumbnail may skip over keys. If it's too steep, it may dig in and produce uneven notes. Experiment with the angle until you find the sweet spot where each note sounds clearly.
### Using the Back of the Fingers for Ascending
For an ascending white-key glissando (right hand, low to high), the most common technique uses the back of the second, third, or fourth finger. Turn your hand palm-down, and use the nail-side of the finger (the dorsal surface) to slide across the keys.
The motion comes from the arm, not just the finger. The arm moves in a smooth, sweeping motion across the keyboard, with the finger maintaining contact with the keys throughout. The wrist should be flexible, allowing the hand to follow the natural arc of the keyboard.
### Hand Position and Arm Angle
The hand position for a glissando is quite different from normal playing position. For a descending glissando with the thumbnail, the hand is rotated so the palm faces up and the thumb is on the bottom. For an ascending glissando with the back of the finger, the palm faces down and the finger is on top.
The arm should be relatively straight, with the elbow leading the motion. The arm angle should be parallel to the keyboard — if the arm is at an angle, the glissando will be uneven. Keep the shoulder relaxed and let the arm do the work, not the wrist or fingers.
<Note>
**Keep the wrist flexible.** A stiff wrist creates tension and makes the glissando sound jerky. The wrist should act as a shock absorber, allowing the hand to glide smoothly across the keys while maintaining even contact.
</Note>
## Black-Key Glissando Technique
### Using the Pads of the Fingers
Black-key glissandos are more challenging than white-key glissandos because the black keys are narrower and more raised. The technique uses the pads of the fingers (the fleshy part on the palm side) rather than the nails.
For a descending black-key glissando, use the pad of the second or third finger, sliding across the black keys with a light, skimming motion. For an ascending black-key glissando, use the pad of the thumb, sliding upward across the black keys.
### The Challenge of Black-Key Glissandos
Black-key glissandos are more difficult because the narrower keys require greater precision. The finger must stay centered on the black keys — if it drifts to the side, it will hit the white keys and produce unwanted notes. The raised position of the black keys also means the hand must be positioned higher, which can feel awkward.
Additionally, black-key glissandos produce a pentatonic scale (the black keys form a pentatonic pattern), which has a different musical character than the diatonic scale of a white-key glissando. This can be used to musical effect — the pentatonic sound has a distinctly Eastern or bluesy quality.
### Practice Methods for Accuracy
To practice black-key glissandos, start slowly. Play the black keys one at a time, getting a feel for the spacing and the position of your hand. Then gradually increase speed, always maintaining accuracy. If you start hitting white keys, slow down.
Another practice method is to play the glissando with one hand while the other hand plays a chord or note, to get used to the coordination required when glissandos appear in the context of a piece.
## [Building](/blog/how-to-play-piano-tremolo-technique) Speed and Control
### [Starting Slow](/blog/how-to-trill-on-piano-with-control) and Building Tempo
Like any piano technique, glissando speed is built gradually. Start by playing the glissando slowly, ensuring every note sounds clearly and evenly. Then gradually increase speed, always prioritizing evenness over raw speed. A fast but uneven glissando sounds worse than a slower but even one.
Use a metronome to track your speed progress. Set a tempo where you can play the glissando cleanly, and increase by small increments (5 BPM at a time). Don't move to a faster tempo until the current one is completely secure.
### Relaxation to Prevent Injury
Glissandos can be physically demanding, and playing them with tension can lead to injury. The most common injury from glissandos is a sore or blistered thumbnail (from descending glissandos) or sore finger pads (from ascending glissandos). In more serious cases, the repetitive motion can cause strain in the wrist or forearm.
To prevent injury, keep the arm, wrist, and hand relaxed throughout the glissando. Don't press hard into the keys — the weight of the arm provides enough force to depress the keys. If you feel pain or discomfort, stop immediately and rest. Build up endurance gradually, just as you would for any physical activity.
### Dynamic Control During Glissandos
A glissando doesn't have to be a single, uniform volume. You can start softly and build to a loud finish (crescendo glissando), or start loud and fade to nothing (diminuendo glissando). This dynamic control adds musical expression to what could otherwise be a purely technical effect.
To control dynamics, vary the speed and weight of the arm. A faster, heavier arm produces a louder glissando; a slower, lighter arm produces a softer one. Practice glissandos with different dynamic shapes to develop this control.
<Callout>
**Warm up before practicing glissandos.** Like any athletic movement, glissandos are easier and safer when your hands are warm. Do some gentle stretching and slow playing before attempting fast glissandos.
</Callout>
## Glissandos in Repertoire
### Ravel and Gershwin Glissando Passages
Ravel's "Alborada del Gracioso" contains some of the most famous glissando passages in the piano repertoire, including rapid white-key and black-key glissandos that require both speed and precision. Gershwin's "Rhapsody in Blue" opens with a iconic ascending glissando that has become one of the most recognizable piano gestures in all of music.
When practicing these passages, start by isolating the glissando and practicing it alone. Then integrate it into the surrounding music, paying attention to the transition into and out of the glissando. The glissando should flow naturally from what comes before and lead smoothly into what comes after.
### Romantic-Era Glissando Effects
In romantic-era music, glissandos often serve as dramatic gestures — a sweep of sound that creates a sense of excitement or grandeur. Chopin's Waltz in E major, Op. 18, contains glissandos in both hands that cascade down the keyboard, creating a dazzling display of pianistic fireworks. Liszt used glissandos extensively in his operatic paraphrases, where they often represent dramatic moments from the source operas.
### Practicing Glissandos from Your Pieces
When you encounter a glissando in your repertoire, follow this practice sequence:
1. Identify the type (white-key or black-key, ascending or descending, which hand)
2. Practice the glissando slowly and in isolation
3. Practice the measures leading into and out of the glissando
4. Integrate the glissando into the full passage
5. Gradually bring the passage up to [performance](/blog/how-to-memorize-piano-pieces-faster) tempo
<Note>
**Don't over-practice glissandos.** Because they put stress on the thumbnail and finger pads, excessive repetition can cause soreness or injury. Practice glissandos in short bursts with rest periods in between, rather than long, continuous sessions.
</Note>
The glissando is a thrilling technique that adds drama, excitement, and virtuosity to piano music. By mastering the proper hand positions, building speed gradually, and practicing safely, you'll be able to execute these dramatic sweeps with confidence and musicality. Remember that the glissando is always in service of the music — let it enhance the emotional impact of the piece, not just showcase your technical ability.
