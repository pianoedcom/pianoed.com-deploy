A prelude is, by definition, a piece that comes before something else. Yet over the centuries, the piano prelude has grown into a genre that stands proudly on its own — a miniature world complete in itself, often lasting just a minute or two. [The prelude](/blog/classical-piano-genres-explained-for-beginners)'s history is a journey through every key, every mood, and nearly every era of keyboard music.
For beginners and enthusiasts alike, preludes are an ideal way into the piano repertoire. They are short enough to grasp in a single sitting yet deep enough to reward a lifetime of return. This guide walks through the form's evolution.
## Bach: The Foundation
The prelude's story begins long before the modern piano, with Johann Sebastian Bach's *Well-Tempered Clavier*. Completed in two books (1722 and 1742), this collection pairs a prelude and a fugue in every major and minor key — 48 pieces in all. Bach wrote them partly to demonstrate the possibilities of tuning systems that allowed music in all keys.
Bach's preludes are astonishingly varied. Some are improvisatory toccatas, others are steady technical studies, and the famous C major prelude of Book 1 is a flowing cascade of broken chords that has inspired composers ever since. Though written for harpsichord and clavichord, they are now central to the piano repertoire.
<Callout title="Why all the keys?">
By writing a prelude and fugue in every key, Bach celebrated the emerging system of equal temperament, which made it possible to play in any key without retuning. The idea of a complete cycle in all 24 keys became a model for centuries of composers.
</Callout>
## [Chopin](/blog/nocturne-piano-music-guide): The Prelude as Character Piece
Frédéric Chopin reinvented the prelude with his Op. 28 set of 24 preludes, one in each major and minor key. Unlike Bach, Chopin did not pair his preludes with fugues — each stands alone as a brief, evocative character piece. Some last under a minute; the famous Prelude in E minor is a tiny tragedy of just a few pages.
Chopin's preludes cover an astonishing emotional range, from the sunny Prelude in D major to the rain-soaked Prelude in E minor (often associated with the nickname "Raindrop," though Chopin never named it). Together, they form a kind of emotional diary, and they remain among the most played and loved piano pieces ever written.
<Tip>Listen to the Op. 28 preludes in order. The cycle has its own architecture, moving through keys and moods in a way that feels like a single, varied journey.</Tip>
## The [Romantic](/blog/piano-four-hand-repertoire-guide) and National Traditions
After Chopin, the prelude became a favored form for composers who wanted to write short, expressive pieces. Alexander Scriabin wrote 24 preludes early in his career, deeply influenced by Chopin. Sergei Rachmaninoff's Preludes, including the famous Prelude in C-sharp minor, are grander and more dramatic, with a Russian sweep that fills the concert hall.
Claude Debussy took the prelude in a wholly new direction with his two books of *Préludes*, each piece titled with a poetic evocation placed at the end of the score rather than the beginning. "The Sunken Cathedral," "Footprints in the Snow," "The Girl with the Flaxen Hair" — Debussy's preludes are tone paintings, impressionist sketches that forever changed how pianists think about color.
## The Modern Prelude
The 20th century kept the prelude alive. Dmitri Shostakovich wrote 24 Preludes and Fugues, Op. 87, directly echoing Bach and becoming a cornerstone of modern Russian piano music. George [Gershwin](/blog/rhapsody-piano-music-guide) composed his own set of preludes blending classical and jazz idioms. Contemporary composers continue to add to the genre, ensuring the prelude remains a living [tradition](/blog/impromptu-piano-music-guide).
## How to Explore Preludes
If you want to dive in, here is a simple path:
1. **Start with Bach's C major Prelude** (WTC Book 1) — simple, flowing, and universally loved.
2. **Move to Chopin's Op. 28** — try No. 4 in E minor or No. 15 in D-flat major.
3. **Explore Debussy's Préludes Book 1** for a completely different sound world.
4. **For advanced players**, sample Rachmaninoff or Shostakovich.
For a broader overview of repertoire across eras, see our [classical piano repertoire by era guide](/blog/classical-piano-repertoire-by-era-guide).
## Small Pieces, Big Worlds
The prelude's magic is its compression. In a few minutes, a great prelude can contain an entire emotional universe. From Bach's architectural genius to Chopin's confessional intimacy to Debussy's shimmering colors, the prelude has proven endlessly adaptable. It is the perfect genre for a quiet evening at the piano — or simply a quiet evening listening.
