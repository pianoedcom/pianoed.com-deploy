# Piano Glossary

Welcome! Whether you're sitting down at the keys for the very first time or brushing up on the basics, this glossary explains piano terms in plain language, with a mental picture or practice tip for each one. Words in a definition that have their own entry are linked, so feel free to follow the trail.

<h2 id="letter-a">A</h2>

<GlossaryItem term="Accelerando" id="accelerando" category="Tempo" aliases="accel.">
  <Definition>
    An Italian word meaning "accelerating." It tells you to gradually play faster until the music says otherwise.
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    Picture a train pulling out of a station: it starts slowly and smoothly gathers speed rather than lurching forward. Keep your hands relaxed as you speed up, because tension is the enemy of fast playing.
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<GlossaryItem term="Accidental" id="accidental" category="Notation">
  <Definition>
    A symbol placed before a note that raises it, lowers it, or cancels a previous change. The three accidentals are the [sharp](#sharp), the [flat](#flat), and the [natural](#natural).
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    Think of a temporary detour sign on a road. It only applies until the end of the current [measure](#measure-bar), then you're back on the usual route set by the [key signature](#key-signature).
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<GlossaryItem term="Action" id="action" category="Piano Anatomy">
  <Definition>
    The system of levers, springs, and [hammers](#hammer) inside a piano that turns the press of a key into a hammer striking a string. Digital pianos mimic this with "weighted" or "hammer-action" keys.
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    It works like a row of dominoes. Your finger pushes the first one, the motion passes through a chain of tiny parts, and the last one delivers the "tap" that makes the sound.
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<GlossaryItem term="Adagio" id="adagio" category="Tempo">
  <Definition>
    An Italian word meaning "at ease." It marks a slow, calm tempo, generally around 66–76 beats per minute.
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    Imagine a slow stroll through a quiet garden with nowhere to be. Give every note room to sing.
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<GlossaryItem term="Allegro" id="allegro" category="Tempo">
  <Definition>
    An Italian word meaning "cheerful." It marks a fast, lively tempo, generally around 120–156 beats per minute.
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    Think of a brisk, happy jog. It's energetic, but you can still keep control of every step.
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<GlossaryItem term="Andante" id="andante" category="Tempo">
  <Definition>
    An Italian word meaning "walking." It marks a moderate, flowing tempo, generally around 76–108 beats per minute.
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    This is your natural walking pace: steady, unhurried, and comfortable. If a piece is marked andante, imagine strolling to a friend's house.
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<GlossaryItem term="Arpeggio" id="arpeggio" category="Technique">
  <Definition>
    A [chord](#chord) whose notes are played one after another instead of all at once. The word comes from the Italian for "harp."
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    Imagine slowly strumming a guitar or running your fingers across harp strings, so the chord unfolds like a ribbon. Try C–E–G–C going up the keyboard to hear a C major arpeggio.
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<h2 id="letter-b">B</h2>

<GlossaryItem term="Bass Clef" id="bass-clef" category="Notation" aliases="F Clef">
  <Definition>
    A [clef](#clef) used for lower notes, usually played by the left hand. Its two dots sit on either side of the F line, which is why it's also called the F clef.
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    Think of it as the basement of the music. Everything down here has a deep, rumbling sound, like the foundation of a house.
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<GlossaryItem term="Beat" id="beat" category="Music Theory">
  <Definition>
    The steady pulse that runs through a piece of music. It's the part you naturally tap your foot to.
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    It's the heartbeat of the music, or the tick of a clock. Notes may be long or short, but the beat keeps going evenly underneath.
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<GlossaryItem term="Black Keys" id="black-keys" category="Piano Anatomy">
  <Definition>
    The shorter, raised keys arranged in alternating groups of two and three. They play the [sharps](#sharp) and [flats](#flat).
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    The black key groups are street signs for navigating the keyboard. The white key just to the left of any two-black-key group is always C.
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<h2 id="letter-c">C</h2>

<GlossaryItem term="Chord" id="chord" category="Music Theory">
  <Definition>
    Three or more notes played at the same time. Chords give music its fullness and emotional color.
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    If single notes are individual paint colors, a chord is those colors blended on the canvas. Play C, E, and G together to make a C major chord.
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<GlossaryItem term="Clef" id="clef" category="Notation">
  <Definition>
    A symbol at the start of each [staff](#staff) that tells you which note each line and space stands for. Piano music mainly uses the [treble clef](#treble-clef) and the [bass clef](#bass-clef).
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    A clef is like the legend on a map. Without it, the dots on the staff are just decoration.
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<GlossaryItem term="Crescendo" id="crescendo" category="Dynamics" aliases="cresc.">
  <Definition>
    A gradual increase in volume. It's often shown as a long hairpin shape that opens up to the right.
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    Imagine slowly turning up a volume knob, or a wave building as it approaches the shore. Save your loudest sound for the end of the crescendo so it has room to grow.
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<h2 id="letter-d">D</h2>

<GlossaryItem term="Damper Pedal" id="damper-pedal" category="Piano Anatomy" aliases="Sustain Pedal, Sustaining Pedal">
  <Definition>
    The right-hand pedal. It lifts the dampers (felt pads) off the strings so notes keep ringing after you release the keys.
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    It's like singing in a stairwell or a cathedral, where the sound lingers and blends together. Press the pedal down just after you play a chord, and lift it when the harmony changes to keep things from turning muddy.
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<GlossaryItem term="Decrescendo" id="decrescendo" category="Dynamics" aliases="Diminuendo, decresc., dim.">
  <Definition>
    A gradual decrease in volume. It's often shown as a hairpin shape that closes to the right.
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    Picture the sun sinking below the horizon or a wave rolling back out to sea. The sound fades away gently, without a sudden drop.
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<GlossaryItem term="Dotted Note" id="dotted-note" category="Notation">
  <Definition>
    A note with a small dot after it. The dot adds half of the note's original length.
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    Think of the dot as a 50% tip on the note's value. A half note lasts 2 beats, so a dotted half note lasts 3 beats (2 plus 1).
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<GlossaryItem term="Dynamics" id="dynamics" category="Dynamics">
  <Definition>
    The loudness and softness of music. Dynamics are shown with markings like [pianissimo](#pianissimo), [piano](#piano), [forte](#forte), and [crescendo](#crescendo).
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    Dynamics are the music's tone of voice, ranging from a whisper to a shout. The same words sound completely different depending on how you say them.
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<h2 id="letter-e">E</h2>

<GlossaryItem term="Expression" id="expression" category="Technique">
  <Definition>
    The emotional shaping of music through [dynamics](#dynamics), flexible timing, touch, pedaling, and phrasing. It's what turns correct notes into real music.
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    Reading a story aloud is a good comparison. The notes are the words, and expression is how you say them: with pauses, emphasis, warmth, or excitement.
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<h2 id="letter-f">F</h2>

<GlossaryItem term="Fermata" id="fermata" category="Notation">
  <Definition>
    A symbol shaped like an arc with a dot underneath, placed over a note or rest. It tells you to hold it longer than its written value, and how much longer is up to you.
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    It's the music's pause button, like a held breath before the next thought. Trust your ear and let the moment breathe.
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<GlossaryItem term="Fingering" id="fingering" category="Technique">
  <Definition>
    The numbers written above or below notes that tell you which finger to use: 1 is the thumb, 2 the index, 3 the middle, 4 the ring, and 5 the pinky.
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    Fingering is a GPS route for your hands. There are many ways to reach the destination, but a good route keeps you smooth and avoids awkward jumps.
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<GlossaryItem term="Flat" id="flat" category="Notation" aliases="♭">
  <Definition>
    A symbol (♭) that lowers a note by one [half step](#half-step), moving to the very next key on the left. It's often, but not always, a [black key](#black-keys).
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    Think of a flat tire sinking lower to the ground. A flat pushes the pitch down.
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<GlossaryItem term="Forte" id="forte" category="Dynamics" aliases="f">
  <Definition>
    An Italian word meaning "strong." It tells you to play loudly.
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    Imagine speaking with confidence to a room full of people: clear and full-bodied, but not shouting. Loud playing should still sound beautiful, never harsh.
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<GlossaryItem term="Fortissimo" id="fortissimo" category="Dynamics" aliases="ff">
  <Definition>
    A marking meaning "very loud." It's one step louder than [forte](#forte).
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    Think of a stadium crowd erupting after a game-winning goal. Use your arm weight rather than tense fingers, and keep the tone full instead of banging.
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<h2 id="letter-g">G</h2>

<GlossaryItem term="Grand Piano" id="grand-piano" category="Piano Anatomy">
  <Definition>
    A piano whose frame, strings, and soundboard lie horizontally, with a large lid on top. Its [action](#action) uses gravity to reset the hammers, which allows very fast repeated notes and a rich, resonant tone.
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    This is the piano you picture on a concert stage. Its strings stretch out horizontally like the wing of a bird, and the lid can be propped open to project the sound into the room.
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<GlossaryItem term="Grand Staff" id="grand-staff" category="Notation">
  <Definition>
    Two [staves](#staff), one with a [treble clef](#treble-clef) and one with a [bass clef](#bass-clef), joined by a brace. It's how piano music is written so both hands can be shown together.
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    Imagine a two-story building: the treble staff is the upstairs floor, and the bass staff is the downstairs floor. [Middle C](#middle-c) sits in the stairwell between them.
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<h2 id="letter-h">H</h2>

<GlossaryItem term="Half Step" id="half-step" category="Music Theory" aliases="Semitone">
  <Definition>
    The smallest distance between two notes on the piano: from one key to the very next key, whether it's black or white. E to F and B to C are half steps with no black key between them.
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    Think of a single stair step. Two of them make a [whole step](#whole-step).
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<GlossaryItem term="Hammer" id="hammer" category="Piano Anatomy">
  <Definition>
    A small felt-covered wooden piece inside the piano that strikes the strings when you press a key. Each key has its own hammer.
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    Picture a tiny drumstick that bounces right back after hitting the string. That quick bounce is what lets the string keep vibrating and singing.
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<GlossaryItem term="Harmony" id="harmony" category="Music Theory">
  <Definition>
    The sound of multiple notes played together, especially the [chords](#chord) that support a [melody](#melody).
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    If the melody is the lead singer, harmony is the band behind them. It gives the tune depth, mood, and direction.
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<h2 id="letter-i">I</h2>

<GlossaryItem term="Interval" id="interval" category="Music Theory">
  <Definition>
    The distance between two notes, named by counting letter names including the starting note. C up to E is a "third" because you count C-D-E.
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    An interval is like the gap between two stairs or the distance between two cities on a map. Learning to recognize common intervals on sight makes reading music far faster.
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<h2 id="letter-k">K</h2>

<GlossaryItem term="Key" id="key" category="Music Theory">
  <Definition>
    The "home base" of a piece of music, named for its main note and [scale](#scale), as in "the key of C major." (It also means a physical key you press, but in music theory it describes the tonal center.)
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    Think of a key as a song's hometown. The music wanders off and explores, but it usually feels most settled when it returns home.
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<GlossaryItem term="Key Signature" id="key-signature" category="Notation">
  <Definition>
    A group of [sharps](#sharp) or [flats](#flat) at the beginning of each [staff](#staff) that tells you which notes to raise or lower throughout the piece and reveals the [key](#key) you're in.
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    It's a standing instruction, like a note on a recipe that says "always use brown sugar." Instead of writing a sharp beside every F♯, the composer states it once at the start.
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<h2 id="letter-l">L</h2>

<GlossaryItem term="Ledger Line" id="ledger-line" category="Notation">
  <Definition>
    A short extra line drawn above or below the [staff](#staff) so notes that are too high or too low can still be written.
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    Ledger lines are extra rungs added to the top or bottom of a ladder. They let you keep climbing beyond the five lines that were already there.
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<GlossaryItem term="Legato" id="legato" category="Technique">
  <Definition>
    An Italian word meaning "tied together." It tells you to play smoothly, with no gaps between the notes.
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    Speak a sentence in one flowing breath without stopping between words. On the piano, release each key just as the next one goes down.
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<h2 id="letter-m">M</h2>

<GlossaryItem term="Major and Minor" id="major-and-minor" category="Music Theory">
  <Definition>
    The two most common types of [scales](#scale), [keys](#key), and [chords](#chord). Major sounds bright and happy, while minor sounds darker and more serious.
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    Think of a sunny day versus a cloudy one. Try playing C–E–G (major), then lower the middle note to C–E♭–G (minor) and listen to the mood shift.
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<GlossaryItem term="Measure (Bar)" id="measure-bar" category="Notation" aliases="Measure, Bar">
  <Definition>
    A section of music between two vertical bar lines. Each measure holds a fixed number of [beats](#beat), set by the [time signature](#time-signature).
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    Measures are like sentences in a paragraph. They break the music into small, readable chunks so you don't lose your place.
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<GlossaryItem term="Melody" id="melody" category="Music Theory">
  <Definition>
    The main tune of a piece: a sequence of single notes that forms the part you'd hum or sing along with.
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    The melody is what you'd hum in the shower. Everything else, like [harmony](#harmony) and accompaniment, is there to support it.
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<GlossaryItem term="Metronome" id="metronome" category="Technique">
  <Definition>
    A device or app that clicks at a steady speed you choose, measured in beats per minute (BPM). It helps you practice keeping an even [tempo](#tempo).
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    A metronome is a tireless drummer who never speeds up or slows down. Start slower than you think you need to, then raise the speed a few BPM at a time as the passage gets easier.
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<GlossaryItem term="Mezzo Forte" id="mezzo-forte" category="Dynamics" aliases="mf">
  <Definition>
    A marking meaning "medium loud." It sits between [forte](#forte) and mezzo piano (moderately soft), and it's one of the most common dynamics in beginner music.
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    Think of a normal, clear speaking voice in a room, present and easy to hear without being raised.
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<GlossaryItem term="Middle C" id="middle-c" category="Piano Anatomy" aliases="C4">
  <Definition>
    The C closest to the center of the keyboard. It's the white key just to the left of the two-black-key group nearest the middle, and it's the landmark that links the [treble](#treble-clef) and [bass](#bass-clef) staves.
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    Middle C is the "You Are Here" pin on your keyboard map. Once you can find it instantly, you can find every other note relative to it.
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<GlossaryItem term="Moderato" id="moderato" category="Tempo">
  <Definition>
    An Italian word meaning "moderate." It marks a medium tempo, generally around 108–120 beats per minute.
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    Picture a steady, unhurried drive down a quiet road: not slow, not fast, just comfortably in control.
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<h2 id="letter-n">N</h2>

<GlossaryItem term="Natural" id="natural" category="Notation" aliases="♮">
  <Definition>
    A symbol (♮) that cancels a [sharp](#sharp) or [flat](#flat), returning the note to its plain version, which is usually a [white key](#white-keys).
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    A natural is the undo button. Whatever change came before, it puts the note back to normal.
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<GlossaryItem term="Note" id="note" category="Notation">
  <Definition>
    A symbol on the [staff](#staff) that tells you two things: which key to play (its position on the staff) and how long to hold it (its shape, such as whole, half, quarter, or eighth).
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    A note is like a short text message with two pieces of information: "play this key" and "hold it this long." Position answers the first, and shape answers the second.
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<h2 id="letter-o">O</h2>

<GlossaryItem term="Octave" id="octave" category="Music Theory">
  <Definition>
    The distance from one note to the next note with the same letter name, higher or lower. They sound like the "same" note at a different height, and on the keyboard, C to the next C is eight white keys.
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    Imagine a man and a woman singing the same tune together, each in their own natural range. It's the same melody at two different heights, and that's how octaves sound.
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<h2 id="letter-p">P</h2>

<GlossaryItem term="Pianissimo" id="pianissimo" category="Dynamics" aliases="pp">
  <Definition>
    A marking meaning "very soft." It's one step quieter than [piano](#piano).
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    Think of whispering in a library or tiptoeing past a sleeping baby. Keep your touch light, but stay in control so every note still sounds.
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<GlossaryItem term="Piano" id="piano" category="Dynamics" aliases="p">
  <Definition>
    An Italian word meaning "soft," marked as a small p. It tells you to play quietly. (The instrument's full name, pianoforte, means "soft-loud" because it could do both.)
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    Imagine speaking gently to someone who's resting nearby. Soft playing is about control, not timidity.
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<GlossaryItem term="Pitch" id="pitch" category="Music Theory">
  <Definition>
    How high or low a note sounds. On the piano, pitch rises as you move right and falls as you move left.
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    Compare a bird's chirp to a bass drum. The chirp has a high pitch and the drum a low one, and your keyboard holds that whole range from left to right.
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<GlossaryItem term="Polyphony" id="polyphony" category="Music Theory">
  <Definition>
    Music made of two or more independent melodies played at the same time. On digital pianos, "polyphony" also means how many notes the instrument can sound at once.
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    It's like several people at a dinner table, each telling their own story, and you can still follow every one. A round like "Row, Row, Row Your Boat" is a simple example.
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<GlossaryItem term="Presto" id="presto" category="Tempo">
  <Definition>
    An Italian word meaning "quick." It marks a very fast tempo, generally around 168–200 beats per minute.
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    Think of a sprint. Practice presto passages slowly first, then bring them up to speed gradually.
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<h2 id="letter-r">R</h2>

<GlossaryItem term="Repeat Sign" id="repeat-sign" category="Notation">
  <Definition>
    A double bar line with two dots that tells you to go back and play a section again. A matching sign facing the other way marks where the repeat begins.
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    It works like the "repeat" button on a music player. The dots are your signal to loop back.
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<GlossaryItem term="Rest" id="rest" category="Notation">
  <Definition>
    A symbol for silence lasting a specific number of [beats](#beat). Each type of [note](#note) has a matching rest.
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    Rests are the pauses in speech that give words meaning. Silence is part of the music, so count it as carefully as the notes.
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<GlossaryItem term="Rhythm" id="rhythm" category="Music Theory">
  <Definition>
    The pattern of long and short notes and silences laid over the steady [beat](#beat).
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    The beat is a heartbeat, and rhythm is the pattern of syllables in a sentence. Try clapping the words of "Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star" and you're clapping its rhythm.
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<GlossaryItem term="Ritardando" id="ritardando" category="Tempo" aliases="rit.">
  <Definition>
    A marking that tells you to gradually slow down. It often appears near the end of a phrase or piece.
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    Imagine coasting to a stop on a bicycle, easing off smoothly instead of slamming the brakes. It's the opposite of [accelerando](#accelerando).
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<h2 id="letter-s">S</h2>

<GlossaryItem term="Scale" id="scale" category="Music Theory">
  <Definition>
    A series of notes arranged in order, going up or down, following a set pattern of [whole steps](#whole-step) and [half steps](#half-step). The C major scale uses only white keys from C to the next C.
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    A scale is a staircase for your fingers, and it's also the alphabet a piece of music is written in. Learn the scale and you know which notes belong in that [key](#key).
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<GlossaryItem term="Sharp" id="sharp" category="Notation" aliases="♯">
  <Definition>
    A symbol (♯) that raises a note by one [half step](#half-step), moving to the very next key on the right. It's often, but not always, a [black key](#black-keys).
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    Think of an arrow pointing up. A sharp pushes the pitch higher.
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<GlossaryItem term="Sight-Reading" id="sight-reading" category="Technique">
  <Definition>
    Playing a piece of music from the page the first time you see it, with no practice beforehand.
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    It's like reading a new book aloud for the very first time. Keep going even if you stumble, and train your eyes to glance ahead so you always know what's coming.
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<GlossaryItem term="Slur" id="slur" category="Notation">
  <Definition>
    A curved line over or under a group of notes with different pitches, telling you to play them smoothly as one [legato](#legato) phrase.
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    A slur is a phrase said in a single breath. Don't confuse it with a [tie](#tie), which connects two notes of the same pitch.
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<GlossaryItem term="Soft Pedal (Una Corda)" id="soft-pedal" category="Piano Anatomy" aliases="Una Corda, Left Pedal">
  <Definition>
    The left-hand pedal, which makes the piano sound softer and slightly more mellow. On a grand piano, it shifts the [action](#action) slightly so the [hammers](#hammer) strike fewer strings.
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    It's like draping a soft cloth over a speaker. The sound becomes warmer and more velvety, not just quieter.
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<GlossaryItem term="Sostenuto Pedal" id="sostenuto-pedal" category="Piano Anatomy" aliases="Middle Pedal">
  <Definition>
    The middle pedal on many grand pianos. It sustains only the notes whose keys are already held down when you press it, while notes played afterward are not sustained. On many upright pianos, the middle pedal does something different, such as muting the sound for quiet practice.
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    Think of it as a selective freeze-frame. It holds some notes in place while the rest of the music keeps moving.
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<GlossaryItem term="Staccato" id="staccato" category="Technique">
  <Definition>
    An Italian word meaning "detached." It tells you to play notes short and separated, and it's usually marked with a small dot above or below the note.
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    Touch each key as if it were a hot stove: press and lift quickly. It's the opposite of [legato](#legato).
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<GlossaryItem term="Staff" id="staff" category="Notation" aliases="Stave, Staves">
  <Definition>
    The set of five horizontal lines (and the four spaces between them) on which music is written. The higher a note sits on the staff, the higher it sounds.
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    The staff is lined paper for music. Just as handwriting sits on the lines, notes sit on lines and spaces, and their height tells you the [pitch](#pitch).
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<GlossaryItem term="Sustaining Pedal" id="sustaining-pedal" category="Piano Anatomy" aliases="Sustain Pedal">
  <Definition>
    Another name for the [damper pedal](#damper-pedal), the right-hand pedal that lets notes keep ringing after you lift your fingers.
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    The name says it all: it sustains. Notes keep floating in the air like a soap bubble that hasn't popped yet.
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<h2 id="letter-t">T</h2>

<GlossaryItem term="Tempo" id="tempo" category="Tempo">
  <Definition>
    The speed of the music, or how fast the [beat](#beat) moves. It's often described with Italian words like [allegro](#allegro) or [adagio](#adagio), or with beats per minute (BPM).
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    Tempo is the pace of your walk, whether a slow amble or a brisk march. A [metronome](#metronome) can help you find and keep it.
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<GlossaryItem term="Tie" id="tie" category="Notation">
  <Definition>
    A curved line connecting two notes of the same pitch. You play the first note and hold it for the combined length of both.
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    Picture two train cars coupled together into one long car. You only "press" once, but the sound lasts for both.
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<GlossaryItem term="Time Signature" id="time-signature" category="Notation">
  <Definition>
    Two stacked numbers at the start of a piece. The top number tells you how many [beats](#beat) are in each [measure](#measure-bar), and the bottom number tells you which type of note gets one beat.
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    Think of a recipe: the top number is how many cups, and the bottom number is the size of the cup. In 4/4, you have four quarter-note beats in every measure.
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<GlossaryItem term="Treble Clef" id="treble-clef" category="Notation" aliases="G Clef">
  <Definition>
    A [clef](#clef) used for higher notes, usually played by the right hand. Its spiral curls around the G line, which is why it's also called the G clef.
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    Think of it as the upstairs floor of the music, where the brighter, higher sounds live.
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<GlossaryItem term="Triad" id="triad" category="Music Theory">
  <Definition>
    The most basic type of [chord](#chord), made of three notes: a root, a third above it, and a fifth above it. Major, minor, diminished, and augmented triads are the most common types.
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    Imagine three stacked blocks with one key skipped between each: for example, C, E, and G. Once you can build a triad on any note, you've unlocked the foundation of most songs.
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<h2 id="letter-u">U</h2>

<GlossaryItem term="Una Corda" id="una-corda" category="Piano Anatomy" aliases="Soft Pedal">
  <Definition>
    An Italian term meaning "one string." It refers to the [soft pedal](#soft-pedal), because on early pianos it made the hammers strike just one string per note. The marking "tre corde" ("three strings") tells you to release the pedal.
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    When you see una corda in the score, think "softer, with a gentler color." It changes the tone as well as the volume.
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<GlossaryItem term="Upright Piano" id="upright-piano" category="Piano Anatomy" aliases="Vertical Piano">
  <Definition>
    A piano whose strings and soundboard are arranged vertically, so it stands against a wall and takes up much less room than a [grand piano](#grand-piano). It's the most common type of acoustic piano in homes and schools.
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    Imagine a grand piano stood on its end to save floor space. It's compact, practical, and perfect for daily practice.
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<h2 id="letter-w">W</h2>

<GlossaryItem term="White Keys" id="white-keys" category="Piano Anatomy" aliases="Natural Keys">
  <Definition>
    The longer, lower keys on the piano that play the seven natural notes: A, B, C, D, E, F, and G. That pattern repeats across the entire keyboard.
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    The white keys are a short alphabet that starts over every seven letters. Learn A through G once, and you can find them everywhere.
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>

<GlossaryItem term="Whole Step" id="whole-step" category="Music Theory" aliases="Whole Tone">
  <Definition>
    A distance of two [half steps](#half-step): you skip exactly one key in between. C to D is a whole step because C♯ sits between them.
  </Definition>
  <Analogy>
    Think of taking two stair steps at once. Scales are built from a specific pattern of these long and short steps.
  </Analogy>
</GlossaryItem>
